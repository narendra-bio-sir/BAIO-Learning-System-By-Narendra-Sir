Board Important — High-Priority Topics Blueprint
Topics ranked by recurring weightage, with the concepts and diagrams examiners expect.

⭐⭐⭐ Top priority – almost always asked

  • Lac operon (5 M) – draw and label i, p, o, z, y, a; explain regulation in the absence of the inducer (repressor binds operator → negative regulation) and in its presence (allolactose inactivates the repressor → transcription proceeds). Name Jacob and Monod, 1961.
  • Meselson-Stahl experiment (5 M) – 15NH4Cl, CsCl density gradient centrifugation, hybrid DNA at 20 min, hybrid + light in equal amounts at 40 min. State clearly that 15N is NOT radioactive.
  • Hershey-Chase experiment (5 M) – 35S labels protein, 32P labels DNA, blending, centrifugation, conclusion that DNA enters the bacterium.
  • Salient features of the Watson-Crick model (5 M) – two antiparallel chains, complementary base pairing with 2 and 3 H-bonds, right-handed coil, pitch 3.4 nm, 10 bp/turn, 20 Å diameter, base stacking.
  • Salient features of the genetic code (5 M) – triplet, unambiguous, degenerate, universal, commaless, AUG dual function, three stop codons.

⭐⭐ High priority – frequently asked

  • DNA fingerprinting methodology (5 M) – Alec Jeffreys, VNTR probes, restriction digestion → electrophoresis → Southern blotting → hybridisation → autoradiography, plus three applications.
  • Salient features of the HGP (5 M) – 3 × 109 bases, ~30,000 genes, dystrophin 2.4 Mb, <2% coding, chromosome 1 (2968) vs Y (231), SNPs at 1.4 million sites.
  • Transcription in eukaryotes (3 M) – three RNA polymerases and hnRNA processing by capping, tailing and splicing.
  • Mechanism of DNA replication (3 M) – origin, replication fork, 5′→3′ polymerisation, leading vs lagging strand, Okazaki fragments, DNA ligase.
  • Griffith's experiment (3 M) – S and R strains, the four injections, and the conclusion (transforming principle, chemical nature unknown).
  • Packaging of DNA (3 M) – the 2.2 m calculation, histone octamer, nucleosome (~200 bp), beads-on-string, NHC proteins.

⭐ Two-mark favourites – learn as clean pairs

  • Nucleoside vs nucleotide · template strand vs coding strand · exon vs intron.
  • Capping vs tailing · leading strand vs lagging strand · euchromatin vs heterochromatin.
  • Inducible vs repressible operon · point mutation vs frameshift mutation · DNA vs RNA.
  • Why DNA is more stable than RNA · why both DNA strands are not transcribed · properties of a genetic material.
  • Translational unit and UTRs · ribozyme (23S rRNA) · adaptor molecule (tRNA).
Answer-writing tip: for every 5-mark experiment, the examiner expects five numbered points – organism/material used, the labelling or treatment, the observation, the control, and the conclusion. Write them as (i)–(v), not as a paragraph.
Never lose these marks: always write the year and the scientists (Griffith 1928, Avery-MacLeod-McCarty 1933–44, Hershey-Chase 1952, Meselson-Stahl 1958, Taylor 1958, Jacob & Monod 1961, HGP 1990–2003).

1. DNA: The Genetic Material

  • Nucleoside = base + sugar (N-glycosidic bond); nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate (phosphoester bond); chain joined by 3′-5′ phosphodiester bonds, free phosphate at the 5′ end and free –OH at the 3′ end.
  • Scientists: Meischer (Nuclein) · Wilkins & Franklin (X-ray diffraction) · Watson & Crick (double helix) · Chargaff (A:T = G:C = 1).
  • Double helix: two antiparallel chains, right-handed; A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds); pitch 3.4 nm, 10 bp/turn, 0.34 nm between base pairs, diameter 20 Å; stability from H-bonding + base stacking.
  • Central dogma (Crick): DNA → RNA → Protein. Reverse transcription (Temin & Baltimore, 1970) = Teminism.

2. Packaging of DNA & DNA Organisation

  • Length calculation: 6.6 × 109 bp × 0.34 × 10−9 m = ~2.2 m, versus a nucleus of ~10−6 m.
  • DNA lengths: ΦX174 – 5386 nucleotides; lambda phage – 48502 bp; E. coli – 4.6 × 106 bp; human haploid – 3.3 × 109 bp.
  • Prokaryotes: no nucleus; DNA in large loops in the nucleoid, held by positively charged non-histone proteins.
  • Eukaryotes: histones are basic (rich in lysine & arginine); histone octamer (8 molecules); nucleosome = ~200 bp of DNA on the octamer; nucleosomes → beads-on-string chromatin → chromatin fibre (with NHC proteins) → chromosome at metaphase.
  • Euchromatin = loose, light-staining, active; heterochromatin = dense, dark-staining, inactive.

3. The Search for Genetic Material

  • Griffith (1928): S strain = smooth, coated, virulent; R strain = rough, uncoated, non-virulent. Live R + heat-killed S → mice die, live S recovered → a transforming principle exists (chemical nature unknown).
  • Avery, MacLeod & McCarty (1933–44): proteases and RNases had no effect; DNase abolished transformation → DNA is the transforming principle.
  • Hershey & Chase (1952): 35S labels protein, 32P labels DNA; blend + centrifuge; only 32P entered the bacteria → DNA is the genetic material.
  • Properties of genetic material: replicate · be chemically & structurally stable · allow mutation · express as Mendelian characters.
  • Why DNA is more stable: no reactive 2′-OH · thymine instead of uracil · double-stranded and repairable · RNA is a reactive biocatalyst. RNA evolved first; DNA evolved from RNA. RNA is genetic material in TMV and Qβ bacteriophage.

4. DNA Replication

  • Semi-conservative – each daughter DNA has one parental + one new strand; proposed by Watson & Crick (1953); occurs in the S phase.
  • Meselson & Stahl (1958): E. coli in 15NH4Cl → shifted to 14N → CsCl density gradient; hybrid DNA at 20 min, equal hybrid + light at 40 min. 15N is NOT radioactive.
  • Taylor (1958): radioactive thymidine in Vicia faba → chromosomal DNA also replicates semi-conservatively.
  • Enzymes: DNA polymerase (5′→3′ only), helicase, topoisomerase, RNA primase, DNA ligase. dNTPs = substrate + energy source. Start at the origin of replication; a replication fork forms.
  • Leading strand continuous (3′→5′ template); lagging strand discontinuous → Okazaki fragments joined by DNA ligase.
  • Rate: E. coli 4.6 × 106 bp in 38 min → ~2000 bp/sec. Failure of cell division after replication → polyploidy.

5. Transcription

  • Transcription unit = promoter + structural gene + terminator. Promoter is upstream (5′) and terminator downstream (3′) of the coding strand.
  • Template strand = 3′→5′ (antisense, actually read); coding strand = 5′→3′ (sense, not transcribed, same as mRNA with T for U) – a misnomer.
  • Both strands are not transcribed because (i) one gene would give two different proteins and (ii) the two complementary RNAs would form untranslatable dsRNA.
  • Cistron = DNA coding for one polypeptide. Eukaryotes: monocistronic, split genes (exons = expressed; introns = transcribed but not translated). Prokaryotes: polycistronic.
  • RNA types: mRNA (template) · tRNA (adaptor) · rRNA (structural + catalytic).
  • Steps: initiation (sigma factor + promoter) → elongation → termination (rho factor).
  • Eukaryotic polymerases: Pol I → rRNA (28S, 18S, 5.8S); Pol II → hnRNA; Pol III → tRNA, 5S rRNA, snRNA. Prokaryotes: one polymerase, transcription and translation coupled.
  • hnRNA processing: capping (methyl guanosine triphosphate, 5′ end), tailing (200–300 adenylate residues, 3′ end), splicing (introns out, exons joined).

6. Genetic Code & tRNA

  • Gamow: 41 = 4 and 42 = 16 are too few for 20 amino acids; 43 = 64 → the code is a triplet. Khorana (synthetic RNA), Nirenberg (cell-free systems), Ochoa (polynucleotide phosphorylase).
  • Features: triplet · unambiguous · degenerate · universal · commaless; AUG = start + methionine. 61 codons code amino acids, 3 stop: UAA (ochre), UAG (amber), UGA (opal) = nonsense/terminator codons.
  • Exceptions: in Paramecium and some ciliates UAA and UGA code for glutamine; in human mitochondria AGA/AGG = stop, UGA = tryptophan, AUA = methionine.
  • Point mutation: single base pair change – sickle-cell anaemia (CTC → CAC, Glu → Val in the beta-globin chain). Frameshift: insertion/deletion; adding or deleting three bases restores the frame – the proof that the codon is a triplet.
  • tRNA (adaptor, postulated by Crick): anticodon loop + amino acid acceptor end (3′); clover-leaf secondary, inverted-L 3D. Initiator tRNA carries methionine and pairs with AUG. No tRNAs for stop codons.

7. Translation & Regulation of Gene Expression

  • Steps: activation (ATP) → charging/aminoacylation of tRNA → initiation (small subunit at AUG + initiator tRNA, then large subunit) → elongation (peptide bond by 23S rRNA = ribozyme; ribosome moves 5′→3′) → termination (release factor).
  • Ribosome = rRNA + ~80 proteins, in two subunits. Translational unit = start codon to stop codon; UTRs (5′ and 3′) are untranslated but needed for efficient translation.
  • Levels of regulation: transcriptional · processing (splicing) · transport of mRNA · translational. In prokaryotes, mainly the rate of transcriptional initiation.
  • Operon = polycistronic gene + common promoter + regulatory genes. Inducible (catabolic, e.g. lac) vs repressible (anabolic, e.g. trp).
  • Lac operon (Jacob & Monod, 1961): i = repressor, p = promoter, o = operator, z = β-galactosidase, y = permease, a = transacetylase.
  • No lactose: repressor binds the operator → z, y, a not transcribed = negative regulation. With lactose/allolactose: the inducer inactivates the repressor → RNA polymerase transcribes → lactose is metabolised.
  • Trp operon: normally ON; structural genes trp E, D, C, B, A; aporepressor (from trp R) + corepressor tryptophan = active repressor → operator blocked = feedback repression. Leader sequence (L) and attenuator (A) fine-tune it.

8. Human Genome Project & DNA Fingerprinting

  • HGP: 13 years, 1990–2003; US Dept of Energy + NIH, later the Wellcome Trust (UK). Goals: identify ~20,000–25,000 genes, sequence 3 billion bp, store data, transfer technology, address ELSI.
  • Cost: $3 per bp → ~US $9 billion; data would fill 3300 books of 1000 pages → hence bioinformatics.
  • Firsts: Haemophilus influenzae (first genome) · Caenorhabditis elegans (first animal) · Arabidopsis (first plant) · Drosophila (2000) · chromosome 1 (May 2006, last of 24).
  • Methods: ESTs (expressed regions only) vs sequence annotation (whole genome, then assign function). Vectors BAC/YAC; hosts bacteria and yeast; Sanger automated sequencing; alignment by overlaps. Maps by restriction polymorphism and microsatellites.
  • Salient features: 3 × 109 (3164.7 million) bases · average gene 3000 bases · largest gene dystrophin, 2.4 Mb · ~30,000 genes · >50% of unknown function · <2% codes for protein · chromosome 1 has most genes (2968), Y the fewest (231) · SNPs at ~1.4 million sites.
  • DNA fingerprinting: 99.9% of sequences identical, 0.1% unique. Satellite DNA forms a smaller peak in density gradient centrifugation (micro- and mini-satellites). Polymorphism arises in non-coding DNA because such mutations do not affect reproductive ability.
  • Method (Alec Jeffreys, VNTR probes, 0.1–20 kb): isolate DNA → cut with restriction endonucleases → gel electrophoresis → Southern blotting onto nylon/nitrocellulose → hybridise with VNTR probe → autoradiography. Unique to every individual except identical twins.
  • Applications: forensic science · paternity/parentage testing · genetic diversity and evolutionary studies.
