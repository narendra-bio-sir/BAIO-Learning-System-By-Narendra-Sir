Board Important — Repeated Question Bank
Categorized model answers and value points for the most-repeated board questions.

⭐⭐⭐ Top priority – almost always asked

  • Miller-Urey experiment (5 M) – draw the spark-discharge apparatus; label the flask with CH4, NH3, H2 in 2:1:2 ratio, the boiling-water flask, the electrodes (simulating lightning at ~800°C) and the condenser/trap. State the observation – amino acids and simple organic compounds (HCN, fatty acids, urea) were formed – and the inference that chemical evolution of life is possible.
  • Darwin's theory of natural selection (5 M) – state the postulates in order: overproduction, limited resources, struggle for existence, variation, survival of the fittest (Herbert Spencer)/natural selection (Darwin), inheritance of useful variations, formation of new species. Mention Malthus's influence and On the Origin of Species (1859).
  • Origin and evolution of man (5 M) – sequence: Dryopithecus/Ramapithecus → Australopithecus (Homo habilis, 2 mya) → Homo erectus (Java man, Peking man; 1.5 mya, ~900cc) → Homo neanderthalensis (1,00,000–40,000 yrs, 1400cc) → Homo sapiens (modern man). Give brain capacities and key discoverers.
  • Evidences of evolution (5 M) – paleontological (fossils), embryological (biogenetic law), morphological/anatomical (homologous vs analogous organs with examples), biochemical, and biogeographical (Darwin's finches, Australian marsupials).
  • Hardy-Weinberg principle (5 M) – state p + q = 1 and p2 + 2pq + q2 = 1; explain genetic equilibrium and the five disturbing factors (gene migration, genetic drift, mutation, genetic recombination, natural selection). Be ready to solve a numerical.

⭐⭐ High priority – frequently asked

  • Theories of origin of life (3 M) – Special Creation (Father Suarez), Cosmozoic/Panspermia (Hermann Richter), Abiogenesis (Aristotle), Biogenesis (Redi, Spallanzani, Pasteur's swan-neck flask) – know who disproved whom and why.
  • Chemical evolution of life – Oparin-Haldane theory (3 M) – reducing atmosphere, formation of simple → complex organic molecules, coacervates (Oparin) and microspheres (Sydney Fox) as protobiont models.
  • Lamarckism (3 M) – principles (influence of environment, use and disuse of organs, inheritance of acquired characters), giraffe-neck example, and Weismann's decaudalisation experiment (22 generations, tail-less rats still produced tailed progeny) that disproved it. Germplasm theory as its corollary.
  • Types of natural selection (3 M) – stabilizing (plant height), directional (adaptive melanism / peppered moth), disruptive (rabbit fur colour) – draw the three bell-curve diagrams.
  • Adaptive radiation (3 M) – Darwin's finches on the Galapagos Islands (beak variation by diet) and Australian marsupials (radiating from a single ancestral stock); convergent evolution of placental wolf and Tasmanian wolf.
  • Mutation theory of Hugo de Vries (2–3 M) – work on Oenothera lamarckiana (evening primrose), saltation (single-step large mutation), and the comparison table: Darwinian variation (minor, directional, gradual) vs mutation (large, random, saltatory).

⭐ Two-mark favourites – learn as clean pairs

  • Homologous organs vs analogous organs · divergent evolution vs convergent evolution.
  • Big Bang theory (Lemaitre) vs Nebular theory (Kant) · Abiogenesis vs Biogenesis.
  • Lamarckism vs Darwinism · Darwinian variation vs mutation (de Vries).
  • Adaptive melanism as directional selection · founder effect (bottleneck/Sewall Wright effect).
  • Coacervate theory (Oparin) vs microsphere theory (Sydney Fox) · biogenetic law (Haeckel) – disproved by Karl Ernst von Baer.
Answer-writing tip: for every 5-mark experiment, the examiner expects five numbered points – the apparatus/material, the treatment/procedure, the observation, any control, and the conclusion. Write them as (i)–(v), not as a paragraph.
Never lose these marks: always write the scientist and the year – Lemaitre 1927, Kant 1755, Redi 1688, Spallanzani 1765, Oparin 1924/1938, Sydney Fox 1959, Miller-Urey 1953, Darwin 1859, Weismann's experiment, Hugo de Vries 1901, Hardy & Weinberg 1908.

1. Origin of Universe & Earth

  • Big Bang theory (George Lemaitre, 1927): universe born in a single titanic explosion ~15 billion years ago; matter, energy, space and time all arose at that instant; expansion & cooling gave H and He, which condensed under gravity into galaxies.
  • Nebular theory (Immanuel Kant, 1755): solar system originated ~5 billion years ago from a rotating cloud of hot gases (nebula). Earth formed from a fragment of this nebula, initially at 4000–8000°C.
  • Earth's layering as it cooled: heavier elements (Fe, Ni) sank to form the core; lighter elements (Si, Al) formed the crust; lightest gases (H, He, N, O, C, Ar) escaped to form the atmosphere.
  • Early atmosphere had no free oxygen – water vapour, methane, CO2 and ammonia. UV rays split water into H and O; H2 escaped; the ozone layer formed; vapour condensed as rain to form oceans.
  • Life appeared about 500 million years after earth formed, i.e. roughly 4 billion years ago.

2. Theories of Origin of Life

  • Special Creation (Father Suarez, 1548–1617): based on the Biblical book of Genesis – world created by God in six days; life = "vital spirit". Purely religious, not scientifically testable, so rejected.
  • Cosmozoic/Panspermia theory (Hermann E. Richter, 1865): life reached earth as spores/microorganisms (cosmozoa) preserved inside meteorites. No evidence of extraterrestrial life has been found, so rejected.
  • Abiogenesis/Spontaneous generation (Greek philosophers, ~600 BC, supported by Aristotle): life arose continuously and spontaneously from non-living matter via a "vital force" – e.g. maggots from dead flesh, mice from moist soil.
  • Biogenesis (Francesco Redi, 1688; Spallanzani, 1765; Louis Pasteur): life arises only from pre-existing life. Redi – covered vs open meat jars, no maggots in covered jars, but critics said microbes still arose spontaneously. Spallanzani – boiled & sealed broth stayed sterile, but critics said boiling destroyed the "vital force" too. Pasteur – swan-necked (S-shaped) flask let air in but trapped dust/microbes in the curve; broth stayed uncontaminated → disproved abiogenesis conclusively, but did not explain how the very first life form arose.

3. Chemical Evolution of Life

  • Most widely accepted theory – proposed independently by Alexander Oparin (1924, "The Origin of Life") and J.B.S. Haldane (English biologist). Life originated from chemicals through gradual progressive change: inorganic → simple organic → colloidal systems → protobiont (first form of life).
  • Prebiotic atmosphere was a reducing environment (no free O2). Simple atoms (H, N, C, O) combined as earth cooled → simple inorganic molecules (water, methane, ammonia) → energy from lightning, UV, volcanic heat converted these into simple organic molecules dissolved in the "primitive soup".
  • Micromolecules combined by dehydration to form macromolecules, including the first nucleic acid (self-replicating gene) → combined with other molecules to form the first protobionts/protocells/ebionts.
  • Coacervate theory (Oparin, 1938): protein-like substances aggregated with a water envelope, forming non-living coacervates showing some life-like structural/functional properties.
  • Microsphere theory (Sydney Fox, 1959): heated amino acids polymerised into protein-like proteinoids, which aggregated into microspheres – bound by a semi-permeable barrier, showed osmotic behaviour, reacted with chemicals, and could replicate by fission/fragmentation.
  • Miller-Urey experiment (1953): simulated the primitive atmosphere in a spark-discharge apparatus – flask with CH4, NH3, H2 (2:1:2) and water heated & sparked continuously (~800°C, simulating lightning) for a week. Result: amino acids and simple organics (HCN, fatty acids, urea) formed – experimental proof that chemical evolution of life is possible.
  • First organisms were heterotrophs (fermenters). A favourable mutation produced chlorophyll → heterotrophs became autotrophs → O2 released → reducing atmosphere became oxidising → ozone layer formed, protecting life from UV.

4. Evidences of Evolution

  • Paleontological: fossils in successive rock strata show life-forms varied over geological time; ages found by radioactive dating. E.g. Tyrannosaurus rex, Pteranodon, and Archaeopteryx – a connecting link between reptiles and birds (reptilian: teeth, clawed fingers, unfused tail vertebrae; avian: feathers, beak).
  • Embryological: Biogenetic law (Ernst Haeckel) – "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny" (e.g. vestigial gill slits in all vertebrate embryos). Disproved by Karl Ernst von Baer – embryos never pass through the adult stage of another animal.
  • Morphological/anatomical: Homologous organs – same basic structure, different function, common ancestry → divergent evolution (e.g. forelimbs of all mammals; thorn of Bougainvillea & tendril of Cucurbita, both modified stems). Analogous organs – different structure, same function, no common ancestry → convergent evolution (e.g. wings of butterfly & bird; eyes of octopus & mammal; flippers of penguin & dolphin; jaws of rabbit (bony) & cockroach (chitinous)).
  • Biochemical: similarities in proteins, genes, other biomolecules and metabolic pathways across related organisms.
  • Natural selection by anthropogenic action – adaptive/industrial melanism (studied by Fisher & Ford): before industrialisation, lichen-covered white tree trunks favoured white Biston betularia; after industrialisation, soot-blackened trunks favoured the dark B. carbonaria. Similarly – pesticide/herbicide-resistant pests and antibiotic-resistant bacteria are living evidence of ongoing evolution.
  • Biogeographical: adaptive radiation of Darwin's finches (Galapagos Islands, beak shape varies by diet) and Australian marsupials (radiating within an isolated continent); placental and marsupial mammals in different continents show convergent evolution (e.g. placental wolf & Tasmanian wolf).

5. Lamarckism (Inheritance of Acquired Characters)

  • Proposed by Jean Baptist Lamarck (French biologist), first to explain organic evolution; published in Philosophie Zoologique (1809).
  • Principles: influence of environment; use and disuse of organs; inheritance of acquired characters.
  • Use of organs: giraffes developed long necks by repeatedly stretching to reach high leaves. Disuse of organs: total disappearance of limbs in snakes.
  • Such "acquired" characters were claimed to pass to offspring – but this was disproved once it was shown that inheritance occurs only through genes.
  • Weismann's decaudalisation experiment: cut the tails of rats over 22 generations and crossed them – every generation still produced tailed progeny, proving acquired characters (tail-lessness) are not inherited.
  • Germplasm theory (Weismann): the body has somatoplasm (all organs except gonads) and germplasm (gonads only); only changes in the germplasm pass to offspring. Confirmed by Castle & Philips (ovary transplant experiment in Guinea pigs). Neo-Lamarckists: Paul Kammerer, Guyer, Smith, McDougall.

6. Darwinism – Theory of Natural Selection

  • Proposed by Charles Darwin after his voyage on H.M.S. Beagle; Alfred Wallace independently reached the same conclusion in the Malay Archipelago. Published as On the Origin of Species (1859).
  • Postulates (in order): overproduction → limited natural resources → struggle for existence → variations → survival of the fittest (Herbert Spencer) / natural selection (Darwin) → inheritance of useful variations → formation of new species.
  • Thomas Malthus's essay on population (1798) influenced Darwin – population grows geometrically, food only arithmetically.
  • Types of natural selection: Stabilizing – average phenotype favoured, both extremes reduced (e.g. medium plant height). Directional – one extreme favoured, the peak shifts (e.g. dark moths after industrialisation). Disruptive – both extremes favoured over the average (e.g. black & white rabbits favoured over grey, on a background of black and white rocks).
  • Criticisms: does not explain inheritance of small non-useful variations, over-specialised organs (elephant tusk, deer antlers), presence of non-functional vestigial organs, the "arrival" of the fittest, degeneration of organs, or sudden discontinuous variation. Darwin's main handicap was lack of knowledge of heredity.

7. Mutation Theory & the Hardy-Weinberg Principle

  • Mutation theory (Hugo de Vries, 1901): based on his work on the evening primrose (Oenothera lamarckiana) – variation arises through mutation, which he called saltation (a single-step large mutation causing speciation).
  • Darwinian variation vs mutation: Darwinian variation is minor, directional and produces gradual evolution; mutation is large, random/sudden/directionless and produces speciation by saltation.
  • Hardy-Weinberg principle: allele frequencies in a population remain constant across generations (genetic equilibrium) unless disturbed. For a gene with alleles A and a at frequencies p and q: p + q = 1, and genotype frequencies follow p2 + 2pq + q2 = 1 (AA : Aa : aa).
  • Equilibrium is disturbed by: gene flow/migration, genetic drift (chance, due to non-random mating), mutation, genetic recombination, and natural selection. A deviation from the expected p2:2pq:q2 ratio signals evolutionary change.
  • Founder effect (Bottleneck effect / Sewall Wright effect): when allele frequency in a small new sample population drifts so much it becomes a distinct species, with the original population becoming the "founder".

8. Origin & Evolution of Man

  • Dryopithecus (Proconsul, ~15 mya) – found in Africa & Europe, ape-like, believed common ancestor of man and apes. Ramapithecus (~15 mya) – found in the Shivalik hills of India by Edward Lewis (1932), more man-like.
  • Australopithecus (~2–3.9 mya) – East African grasslands, first bipedal hominid, ate mostly fruit, brain 500–700 cc. "Lucy" (A. afarensis, 3.2 mya) found by Donald Johanson (1981); Tuang baby found near Tuang, Africa.
  • Homo habilis ("handy man", tool-maker, brain 650–800 cc) – discovered by L.S.B. & Mary Leakey (1960) in Olduvai Gorge, East Africa.
  • Homo erectus (brain ~900 cc, ate meat, lived ~1.5 mya) – three fossil forms: Java man (Pithecanthropus erectus, Eugene Dubois 1891), Peking man (Sinanthropus pekinensis, W.C. Pei 1924, near Beijing), and Heidelberg man.
  • Homo neanderthalensis (brain ~1400 cc, near east & central Asia, 1,00,000–40,000 years ago) – used hides, buried their dead.
  • Homo sapiens arose in Africa during the ice age (75,000–10,000 years ago) and spread across continents. Cro-Magnon man (Homo sapiens fossilis, France, ~34,000 years ago) is regarded as the most recent, direct ancestor of modern humans; famous for cave paintings.
  • Cranial capacities: Rhesus monkey 200 cc · Orang-utan 375 cc · Chimpanzee 380 cc · Gorilla 500 cc · modern Homo sapiens ~1450 cc.
