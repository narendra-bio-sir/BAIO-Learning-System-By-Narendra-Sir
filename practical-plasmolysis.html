Plasmolysis in a Rhoeo Leaf

Rhoeo (Tradescantia spathacea) epidermal cells carry a deep pink pigment dissolved right in their cell sap — which makes osmosis visible under a plain compound microscope. Press play to watch a strong salt solution pull the living protoplast away from its wall, and a rinse of water push it back.

Why it happens

The cell wall is rigid and freely permeable — it holds its shape and lets any liquid pass straight through. Just inside it, the plasma membrane is selectively permeable: water crosses it far more easily than dissolved salt or sugar does.

Dropped into a solution stronger than the cell sap, water leaves the vacuole by osmosis and the protoplast shrinks away from the wall — plasmolysis. Rinsed back into plain water, the now-concentrated cell sap pulls water in again and the protoplast swells until it presses against the wall once more — deplasmolysis. That recovery is the proof the cell was alive throughout.

Method

You need: a fresh Rhoeo leaf · watch glass · strong salt or sugar solution · slide & coverslip · dropper · blotting paper · compound microscope · distilled water

  1. Peel the lower epidermis and mount it in a drop of water.
  2. Focus under the microscope — cells look turgid and evenly pigmented.
  3. Drip strong salt solution at one edge of the coverslip; draw it through with blotting paper.
  4. Watch the protoplast round off and pull clear of the wall.
  5. Draw distilled water through the same way and watch the cell recover.
