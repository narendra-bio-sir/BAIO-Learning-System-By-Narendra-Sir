Benedict's Test for Reducing Sugars

Benedict's reagent turns from blue to brick-red when it meets a reducing sugar like glucose — and heating is what drives the reaction. Press play to add the unknown sample, heat the tube in a water bath, and watch the colour march from blue through green and yellow to orange, and finally to a brick-red precipitate.

Aim

To test a given unknown sample solution for the presence of reducing sugars (such as glucose) using Benedict's test.

Principle

Benedict's reagent is an alkaline solution of copper sulphate, sodium carbonate and sodium citrate — a deep blue liquid because of its Cu²⁺ ions. A reducing sugar (one with a free aldehyde or ketone group, such as glucose, fructose, maltose or lactose) reduces these Cu²⁺ ions to Cu⁺ on heating.

The Cu⁺ ions combine with hydroxide ions to form insoluble, brick-red cuprous oxide (Cu₂O), which settles out as a precipitate. Non-reducing sugars, such as sucrose, have no free aldehyde/ketone group and so give a negative test unless first hydrolysed into their reducing components.

Method

Requirements

You need: unknown sample solution · Benedict's reagent · clean test tubes · dropper · water bath / beaker of boiling water · test tube holder

Procedure

  1. Take 2 mL of Benedict's reagent in a clean test tube.
  2. Add 8–10 drops of the unknown sample solution and mix.
  3. Place the tube in a beaker of boiling water for 2–3 minutes.
  4. Remove the tube carefully with a holder and let it cool slightly.
  5. Note the colour change or precipitate formed.

Inference
  • An orange or brick-red precipitate in the unknown sample after heating confirms the presence of a reducing sugar such as glucose.
  • No colour change (the solution stays Benedict's-reagent blue) means the sample does not contain a reducing sugar — it may still contain a non-reducing sugar like sucrose.
  • The exact colour reached — green, yellow, orange or brick-red — roughly indicates how much reducing sugar is present, from a trace up to a high concentration.
Precautions
  • Heat the tube gently and evenly in a water bath rather than directly over a flame, to avoid the liquid spurting out.
  • Use a test tube holder while removing the hot tube from the water bath.
  • Do not confuse this with the Biuret test — Benedict's test needs heat, while Biuret works at room temperature.
