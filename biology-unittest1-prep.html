Chapter 1 · The Living WorldBoard 2026–27: 2 marks · 2×1mFull notes · current NCERT text
1.1 Diversity in the Living World ▸
  • Each different kind of plant, animal or organism represents a species. The number of species known and described is 1.7–1.8 million — this is biodiversity, the number and types of organisms on earth. New organisms are still being identified.
  • Local names vary from place to place, so naming is standardised so that an organism has the same name all over the world — nomenclature. Naming is possible only when the organism is described correctly and we know which organism the name refers to — identification.
  • Codes: ICBN (International Code for Botanical Nomenclature) for plants; ICZN (International Code of Zoological Nomenclature) for animals. Scientific names ensure each organism has only one name, and that the name is not used for any other organism.
  • Binomial nomenclature (given by Carolus Linnaeus) — each name has two components: the generic name and the specific epithet. E.g. Mangifera indica (mango): Mangifera = genus, indica = specific epithet.
  • Universal rules: (1) names are in Latin (or Latinised) and in italics; (2) the first word is the genus, the second the specific epithet; (3) when handwritten, both words are separately underlined (or printed in italics); (4) the genus starts with a capital letter, the specific epithet with a small letter. The author's name is written after the specific epithet in abbreviated form, e.g. Mangifera indica Linn. (first described by Linnaeus).
  • Classification — grouping into convenient categories based on easily observable characters (plants, animals, dogs, mammals, wheat…). The scientific term for these categories is taxa; taxa can be at very different levels ('animals', 'mammals', 'dogs').
  • Taxonomy — classifying organisms into taxa based on characteristics. External and internal structure, cell structure, development and ecological information form the basis of modern taxonomy. Characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature are the basic processes of taxonomy. Earliest classifications were based on the 'uses' of organisms.
  • Systematics — from Latin systema, 'systematic arrangement of organisms' (Linnaeus' book: Systema Naturae); studies kinds and diversity of organisms and the relationships among them; later enlarged to include identification, nomenclature and classification; takes into account evolutionary relationships.
1.2 Taxonomic Categories & Hierarchy ▸

Classification is a hierarchy of steps; each step is a rank or category (a unit of classification), called a taxon (pl. taxa). All categories together form the taxonomic hierarchy. Taxa are distinct biological entities, not merely morphological aggregates. Species is the lowest category for all organisms.

  • Species — a group of individuals with fundamental similarities, distinguishable from closely related species by distinct morphological differences. E.g. indica, tuberosum, leo are specific epithets. Panthera also has tigris; Solanum includes nigrum and melongena. Man: Homo sapiens.
  • Genus — a group of related species with more characters in common than with species of other genera. Potato and brinjal: genus Solanum. Lion (P. leo), leopard (P. pardus), tiger (P. tigris): genus Panthera, which differs from genus Felis (cats).
  • Family — a group of related genera with still fewer similarities; in plants based on vegetative and reproductive features. Solanum, Petunia, Datura: family Solanaceae. Panthera and Felis: family Felidae; dogs belong to Canidae.
  • Order — an assemblage of families showing a few similar characters. Convolvulaceae and Solanaceae: order Polymoniales (mainly floral characters). Felidae and Canidae: order Carnivora.
  • Class — related orders. Order Primata (monkey, gorilla, gibbon) and order Carnivora (tiger, cat, dog): class Mammalia.
  • Phylum — classes of fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals share the notochord and dorsal hollow neural system: phylum Chordata. In plants the equivalent category is Division.
  • Kingdom — the highest category: all animals in Kingdom Animalia, all plants in Kingdom Plantae.
  • Rule of the hierarchy — going up from species to kingdom, the number of common characteristics decreases. The lower the taxon, the more characteristics its members share; the higher the category, the harder it is to determine relationships with other taxa at the same level.
  • Table 1.1 (very important): Man — Homo sapiens, Hominidae, Primata, Mammalia, Chordata. Housefly — Musca domestica, Muscidae, Diptera, Insecta, Arthropoda. Mango — Mangifera indica, Anacardiaceae, Sapindales, Dicotyledonae, Angiospermae. Wheat — Triticum aestivum, Poaceae, Poales, Monocotyledonae, Angiospermae.
Board extra · What Is Living? ▸

Note: this section is not in the current (2026–27) NCERT reprint, but the Karnataka board's own Unit Test 1 Model Question Paper asks it, so learn it.

  • Growth — increase in mass and number of cells. Not a defining property: non-living objects (crystals, mountains, sand mounds) also grow, by accumulation of material on the surface.
  • Reproduction — not a defining property: many organisms (mules, sterile worker bees, infertile human couples) do not reproduce. In unicellular organisms like bacteria and Amoeba, growth and reproduction are the same event (mutually inclusive).
  • Metabolism — the sum total of all chemical reactions in the body; a defining feature of all living organisms without exception. But isolated metabolic reactions done outside the body in a test tube are neither living nor non-living.
  • Cellular organisation of the body is the defining feature of life forms.
  • Consciousness — the ability to sense the surroundings and respond to environmental stimuli (physical, chemical or biological) — shown by all organisms; human beings alone have self-consciousness.

2.1 Five Kingdom Classification & Kingdom Monera ▸

Proposed by R.H. Whittaker (1969), based on cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction and phylogenetic relationships: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia. Viruses, viroids and lichens are left out of all five.

  • Monera — prokaryotic, unicellular. Archaebacteria survive in extreme habitats (methanogens in marshy areas and ruminant guts; halophiles in extreme salt; thermoacidophiles in hot acidic springs) due to a different cell wall structure. Eubacteria (true bacteria) are grouped by nutrition — photosynthetic autotrophs (cyanobacteria / BGA, e.g. Nostoc, Anabaena, some fix atmospheric N2), chemosynthetic autotrophs (oxidise inorganic substances), and heterotrophs (most bacteria — decomposers, a few pathogenic, e.g. citrus canker). Mycoplasma lack a cell wall entirely — the smallest living cells known, many pathogenic.
2.2 Kingdom Protista ▸

Single-celled eukaryotes, mostly aquatic, with a defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

  • Chrysophytes — diatoms and golden algae; photosynthetic; cell walls form indestructible pits (diatomaceous earth).
  • Dinoflagellates — mostly marine, photosynthetic, two flagella (one longitudinal, one transverse), stiff cellulose cell wall plates. The red species Gonyaulax multiplies rapidly to cause red tides, releasing toxins that can kill marine animals.
  • Euglenoids — freshwater, no cell wall (a flexible protein-rich pellicle instead), two flagella. Photosynthetic in sunlight but behave as heterotrophs (predators) when sunlight is unavailable. E.g. Euglena.
  • Slime moulds — saprophytic; the body (plasmodium) creeps over decaying matter engulfing organic material; under adverse conditions forms fruiting bodies with resistant spores dispersed by air.
  • Protozoans — heterotrophic predators or parasites: Amoeboid (Amoeba), Flagellated (Trypanosoma — sleeping sickness), Ciliated (Paramecium), Sporozoans (Plasmodium — malaria).
2.3 Kingdom Fungi ▸

Heterotrophic (saprophytic or parasitic), chitin cell wall, mostly multicellular filaments (hypha, network = mycelium) except unicellular yeast.

  • Phycomycetes — aseptate, coenocytic mycelium; asexual spores in a sporangium; sexual fusion forms a thick-walled zygospore. E.g. Mucor, Rhizopus (bread mould).
  • Ascomycetes — septate mycelium; asexual conidia; sexual ascospores inside sac-like asci. Includes Saccharomyces (yeast) and Penicillium (antibiotics).
  • Basidiomycetes — mushrooms, rusts, smuts; sex organs absent, but fusion of two somatic cells gives a dikaryon, which forms basidia bearing 4 basidiospores each. E.g. Agaricus, Puccinia.
  • Deuteromycetes — “imperfect fungi”; only asexual conidia known; decomposers important in mineral cycling. E.g. Alternaria, Trichoderma.
  • Lichens — a symbiosis of algae (food, via photosynthesis) and fungi (shelter, water/mineral absorption); sensitive indicators of SO2 pollution.
2.4 Viruses & Viroids ▸
  • Viruses — sub-microscopic, non-cellular; genetic material (usually RNA) wrapped in a protein coat (capsid, made of capsomeres); obligate parasites, not alive outside a host cell. E.g. TMV (tobacco mosaic virus).
  • Viroids — discovered by T.O. Diener (1971); smaller than viruses; only free low-molecular-weight RNA, no protein coat. Caused potato spindle tuber disease.

3.1 Algae ▸

Chlorophyll-bearing, simple, thalloid, autotrophic, mostly aquatic — unicellular (Chlamydomonas) to colonial (Volvox) to filamentous (Spirogyra). Three classes, by pigment:

  • Chlorophyceae (green) — grass-green (Chl a & b), fresh water, cellulose wall, starch stored. E.g. Chlamydomonas, Ulothrix, Chara.
  • Phaeophyceae (brown) — marine, fucoxanthin pigment, mannitol/laminarin stored, cellulose wall usually covered by a gelatinous coating of algin. E.g. Fucus, Laminaria, Sargassum.
  • Rhodophyceae (red) — r-phycoerythrin pigment, mostly marine (some at greater depths), floridean starch stored; found in well-lighted surface waters and also at great depths. E.g. Polysiphonia, Gelidium.
3.2 Bryophytes ▸

“Amphibians of the plant kingdom” — live on land but need water for sexual reproduction. No true roots, stem or leaves; attached by rhizoids.

  • The dominant plant body is the haploid gametophyte; multicellular sex organs — male antheridium (biflagellate antherozoids), female archegonium (one egg).
  • Fertilisation gives a zygote that grows into a dependent sporophyte (not free-living), which produces spores by meiosis; spores germinate into a new gametophyte.
  • Liverworts (e.g. Marchantia) — thalloid body; asexual reproduction by gemmae in gemma cups.
  • Mosses (e.g. Funaria, Sphagnum) — spore germinates into a protonema first, which develops the upright leafy gametophore; Sphagnum (peat moss) is used as fuel, packing material and a soil conditioner.
3.3 Pteridophytes ▸

The first terrestrial plants with true vascular tissue and a differentiated root-stem-leaf body; the sporophyte is dominant (unlike bryophytes).

  • Spore-bearing leaves are sporophylls. Homosporous types (e.g. Equisetum) produce one kind of spore; heterosporous types (e.g. Selaginella, Salvinia) produce microspores and megaspores — retaining the female gametophyte on the parent sporophyte here is the evolutionary lead-in to the seed habit seen in gymnosperms and angiosperms.
  • The spore grows into a small, free-living gametophyte called the prothallus, which still needs water for fertilisation.
3.4 Gymnosperms & Angiosperms ▸
  • Gymnosperms — “naked seed” plants; ovules are not enclosed by an ovary. Heterosporous; microsporophylls and megasporophylls are compacted into cones/strobili. Monoecious (both cones on one plant, e.g. Pinus) or dioecious (Cycas). Roots may show mycorrhiza (Pinus) or N2-fixing coralloid roots (Cycas).
  • Angiosperms — flowering plants; seeds enclosed within a fruit. Stamen produces pollen (male gametophyte); pistil's ovary encloses ovules (female gametophyte/embryo sac). Range from the tiny Wolffia to tall Eucalyptus trees (over 100 m); provide food, fodder, fuel and medicines. Classified by cotyledon number into Dicotyledonae (2) and Monocotyledonae (1).

4.1 Basis of Animal Classification ▸
  • Levels of organisation — cellular (Porifera) → tissue (Coelenterata) → organ (Platyhelminthes) → organ-system (most others).
  • Symmetry — asymmetrical (Porifera), radial (Coelenterata, Ctenophora, adult Echinodermata), bilateral (everything else, including Echinodermata larvae).
  • Germ layers — diploblastic (Coelenterata, Ctenophora, two layers) vs triploblastic (three layers, everyone from Platyhelminthes onward).
  • Coelom — acoelomate (Platyhelminthes, no body cavity), pseudocoelomate (Aschelminthes, cavity not fully lined by mesoderm), eucoelomate (Annelida onward, true coelom fully mesoderm-lined).
  • Notochord — present at some stage only in Chordata; its absence defines all the non-chordate phyla.
4.2 Non-Chordates, Phylum by Phylum ▸
  • Porifera — pore-bearing, canal system, choanocytes line the body cavity. E.g. Sycon, Spongilla.
  • Coelenterata (Cnidaria) — nematocysts (stinging cells), polyp and medusa body forms. E.g. Hydra, Obelia, Physalia.
  • Ctenophora — comb plates of cilia for locomotion, bioluminescent. E.g. Ctenoplana, Pleurobrachia.
  • Platyhelminthes — flatworms, acoelomate, many parasitic with hooks/suckers. E.g. Taenia (tapeworm), Fasciola (liver fluke).
  • Aschelminthes (Nematoda) — pseudocoelomate roundworms, sexes separate. E.g. Ascaris, Wuchereria (filarial worm).
  • Annelida — true coelom, metameric segmentation, closed circulatory system. E.g. Pheretima (earthworm), Hirudinaria (leech).
  • Arthropoda — largest phylum; jointed appendages, chitinous exoskeleton, open circulatory system. E.g. Apis (honeybee), Anopheles.
  • Mollusca — second-largest phylum; soft body, muscular foot, radula for feeding. E.g. Pila, Pinctada (pearl oyster), Octopus.
  • Echinodermata — spiny-skinned, exclusively marine, water vascular system for locomotion. E.g. Asterias (starfish), Echinus (sea urchin).
  • Hemichordata — worm-like marine animals, body in proboscis/collar/trunk. E.g. Balanoglossus.
4.3 Chordates, Class by Class ▸

All Chordata share: a notochord at some stage, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, paired pharyngeal gill slits, and a post-anal tail.

  • Urochordata/Cephalochordata — notochord only in the larval tail (Ascidia) or persisting through life (Branchiostoma/Amphioxus).
  • Cyclostomata — jawless, circular sucking mouth, ectoparasitic on fish. E.g. Petromyzon.
  • Chondrichthyes — cartilaginous skeleton, no operculum or swim bladder. E.g. Scoliodon (dogfish), Pristis.
  • Osteichthyes — bony skeleton, operculum present, air bladder for buoyancy. E.g. Hippocampus, Exocoetus (flying fish).
  • Amphibia — moist skin, no scales, three-chambered heart. E.g. Rana (frog), Bufo (toad).
  • Reptilia — dry cornified scales, three-chambered heart (four in crocodiles). E.g. Chelone, Crocodilus.
  • Aves — feathers, beak, four-chambered heart, endothermic. E.g. Corvus, Struthio (ostrich).
  • Mammalia — mammary glands, hair, four-chambered heart, external ear. E.g. Macropus (kangaroo), Ornithorhynchus (egg-laying platypus).

5.1–5.3 Root, Stem & Leaf ▸
  • Root — tap root system from the radicle in dicots (mustard); fibrous roots from the stem base in monocots (wheat); adventitious roots from parts other than the radicle (grass, Monstera, banyan). Functions: absorption, anchorage, food storage, synthesis of plant growth regulators.
  • Regions of the root tip — root cap (thimble-like, protects the apex) → region of meristematic activity → region of elongation → region of maturation (bears root hairs).
  • Stem — ascending axis from the plumule; bears nodes, internodes and terminal/axillary buds; conducts water, minerals and photosynthates; some stems store food, support, protect or propagate vegetatively.
  • Leaf — lateral outgrowth at the node, bearing an axillary bud; parts: leaf base (may bear stipules; sheath in monocots; swollen pulvinus in some legumes), petiole, lamina with midrib and veins.
  • Venation — reticulate (network; dicots) vs parallel (monocots). Simple leaf: incisions do not reach the midrib. Compound: incisions reach the midrib, forming leaflets; a bud is present in the axil of the petiole, not of leaflets. Pinnately compound (leaflets on a rachis, e.g. neem) vs palmately compound (leaflets at the petiole tip, e.g. silk cotton).
  • Phyllotaxy — alternate (china rose, mustard, sunflower), opposite (Calotropis, guava), whorled (Alstonia).
5.4–5.5 Inflorescence & Flower ▸
  • Inflorescence — arrangement of flowers on the floral axis. Racemose: main axis keeps growing, flowers lateral in acropetal succession. Cymose: main axis ends in a flower, flowers in basipetal order.
  • Flower — modified shoot; four whorls on the thalamus: calyx, corolla (accessory), androecium, gynoecium (reproductive); perianth when calyx and corolla are not distinct. Actinomorphic (mustard, datura, chilli), zygomorphic (pea, gulmohur, bean, Cassia), asymmetric (canna).
  • Position of ovary — hypogynous (superior ovary: mustard, china rose, brinjal), perigynous (half-inferior: plum, rose, peach), epigynous (inferior: guava, cucumber, ray florets of sunflower).
  • Aestivation — valvate (Calotropis), twisted (china rose, lady's finger, cotton), imbricate (Cassia, gulmohur), vexillary/papilionaceous (pea, bean: standard > wings > keel).
  • Androecium — stamen = filament + bilobed anther; sterile stamen = staminode. Epipetalous (brinjal), epiphyllous (lily); monoadelphous (china rose), diadelphous (pea), polyadelphous (citrus).
  • Gynoecium — carpel = stigma + style + ovary; apocarpous (lotus, rose) vs syncarpous (mustard, tomato). Placentation: marginal (pea), axile (china rose, tomato, lemon), parietal (mustard, Argemone; false septum), free central (Dianthus, primrose), basal (sunflower, marigold).
5.6–5.9 Fruit, Seed, Floral Formula & Solanaceae ▸
  • Fruit — mature ovary after fertilisation; formed without fertilisation = parthenocarpic. Pericarp: epicarp, mesocarp, endocarp. Drupe (monocarpellary, superior, one-seeded): mango (fleshy edible mesocarp, stony endocarp), coconut (fibrous mesocarp).
  • Dicot seed — seed coat (testa + tegmen), hilum, micropyle, embryo (embryonal axis + two cotyledons, radicle, plumule). Endospermic (castor) vs non-endospermous (bean, gram, pea).
  • Monocot seed (maize) — generally endospermic (orchids non-endospermic); aleurone layer (proteinous); one shield-shaped cotyledon, the scutellum; plumule in the coleoptile, radicle in the coleorhiza.
  • Floral formula — Br bracteate, K calyx, C corolla, P perianth, A androecium, G gynoecium (line below G = superior ovary, above G = inferior); ⊕ actinomorphic; brackets = fusion, line above = adhesion. Mustard (Brassicaceae): ⊕ K2+2 C4 A2+4 G(2).
  • Solanaceae (potato family) — herbs/shrubs; underground stem in potato; leaves alternate, simple, exstipulate, reticulate; flower bisexual, actinomorphic; K(5) persistent, valvate; C(5) valvate; A5 epipetalous; G(2) bicarpellary, obliquely placed, syncarpous, superior, bilocular, axile with swollen placenta; fruit berry or capsule; seeds many, endospermous.
  • Uses — food (tomato, brinjal, potato), spice (chilli), medicine (belladonna, ashwagandha), fumigatory (tobacco), ornamental (petunia).

6.1 The Tissue System ▸

Based on structure and location there are three tissue systems: epidermal, ground (fundamental) and vascular (conducting).

  • Epidermal tissue system — epidermal cells, stomata and epidermal appendages (trichomes and hairs). Epidermis: usually single-layered, parenchymatous cells with a large vacuole; covered by a waxy cuticle that prevents water loss (cuticle absent in roots).
  • Stomata — regulate transpiration and gaseous exchange. Two bean-shaped guard cells enclose the stomatal pore (dumb-bell shaped in grasses); outer walls thin, inner walls (towards the pore) highly thickened; guard cells have chloroplasts. Specialised neighbouring cells are subsidiary cells. Pore + guard cells + subsidiary cells = stomatal apparatus.
  • Root hairs — unicellular elongations of epidermal cells; absorb water and minerals. Trichomes (on the stem) — usually multicellular, branched or unbranched, soft or stiff, may be secretory; help prevent water loss.
  • Ground tissue system — all tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles: parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma. Parenchyma in cortex, pericycle, pith and medullary rays. In leaves the ground tissue is the chloroplast-containing mesophyll.
  • Vascular tissue system — xylem and phloem form vascular bundles. Open: cambium present between xylem and phloem, can form secondary tissues (dicot stems). Closed: no cambium (monocots). Radial: xylem and phloem on different radii, alternately (roots). Conjoint: on the same radius, phloem usually on the outer side (stems and leaves).
6.2 Anatomy of Dicot & Monocot Plants ▸
  • Dicot root (sunflower) — epiblema with unicellular root hairs; parenchymatous cortex; endodermis of barrel-shaped cells with suberin as casparian strips; pericycle (origin of lateral roots and vascular cambium); 2–4 xylem and phloem patches; conjunctive tissue between them; small pith. Pericycle + vascular bundles + pith = stele.
  • Monocot root — similar, but usually more than six (polyarch) xylem bundles; pith large and well developed; no secondary growth.
  • Dicot stem — epidermis with cuticle; cortex = collenchymatous hypodermis + parenchyma + endodermis (starch sheath); pericycle as semi-lunar sclerenchyma patches; medullary rays; vascular bundles in a ring, each conjoint, open, endarch; large pith.
  • Monocot stem — sclerenchymatous hypodermis; many scattered vascular bundles, each with a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath; conjoint and closed; peripheral bundles smaller; phloem parenchyma absent; water-containing cavities in the bundles; large ground tissue.
  • Dorsiventral (dicot) leaf — more stomata on the abaxial (lower) epidermis; mesophyll differentiated into palisade (adaxial, elongated, vertical) and spongy parenchyma (loose, with air spaces); vascular bundles with thick-walled bundle sheath; reticulate venation, so veins vary in thickness.
  • Isobilateral (monocot) leaf — stomata on both surfaces; mesophyll not differentiated; in grasses, bulliform cells (large, empty, colourless) make leaves curl inwards when flaccid to cut water loss; parallel venation gives near-equal vascular bundles.
  • Secondary growth occurs in most dicot roots and stems (because of cambium); monocots lack it.
Chapter 7 · Structural Organisation in AnimalsBoard 2026–27: 3 marks · 1×3mFull notes · current NCERT text
7.1 Tissue, Organ & Organ System ▸
  • In unicellular organisms one cell does everything; in multicellular animals functions are shared by groups of cells. A tissue is a group of similar cells along with intercellular substances performing a specific function.
  • All complex animals have only four basic tissues — epithelial, connective, muscular and neural — organised in specific proportion and pattern. (Tissue details were studied in earlier classes.)
  • Tissues organise into organs (stomach, lung, heart, kidney); e.g. the heart contains all four tissues. Two or more organs performing a common function form an organ system (digestive, respiratory). Cells, tissues, organs and organ systems share work — division of labour.
  • The increasing complexity of organs and organ systems shows a discernible evolutionary trend.
  • Morphology — study of form or externally visible features. Anatomy — study of the morphology of internal organs in animals.
7.2 Frog — General Features & Morphology ▸
  • Frogs live on land and in freshwater; class Amphibia, phylum Chordata. Common Indian frog: Rana tigrina (Indian bullfrog).
  • Cold-blooded / poikilotherms — body temperature varies with the environment.
  • Change colour to hide from enemies (camouflage); this protective colouration is called mimicry.
  • Shelter in deep burrows: summer sleep (aestivation) and winter sleep (hibernation).
  • Skin — smooth, slippery due to mucus, always moist; dorsal side olive green with dark irregular spots, ventral side uniformly pale yellow. The frog never drinks water; it absorbs it through the skin.
  • Body divisible into head and trunk; neck and tail absent. A pair of nostrils above the mouth; bulging eyes covered by a nictitating membrane (protects them in water); a membranous tympanum (ear) on either side of the eyes receives sound.
  • Limbs — for swimming, walking, leaping, burrowing. Hind limbs end in five digits and are larger and more muscular; fore limbs end in four digits. Feet have webbed digits for swimming.
  • Sexual dimorphism — males have sound-producing vocal sacs and a copulatory pad on the first digit of the fore limbs; absent in females.
7.2.2 Frog Anatomy — Digestive, Respiratory & Circulatory Systems ▸
  • Digestive system — alimentary canal is short (carnivore, so intestine reduced): mouth → buccal cavity → pharynx → oesophagus → stomach → intestine → rectum → cloaca. Food is captured by the bilobed tongue. Liver secretes bile, stored in the gall bladder; pancreas produces pancreatic juice.
  • Stomach walls secrete HCl and gastric juices; partially digested food, chyme, passes to the duodenum, which receives bile and pancreatic juice through a common bile duct. Bile emulsifies fat; pancreatic juices digest carbohydrates and proteins; final digestion in the intestine. Absorption by villi and microvilli; undigested waste leaves through the cloaca.
  • Respiration — in water, the skin is the respiratory organ: cutaneous respiration (dissolved O2 diffuses through the skin). On land, the buccal cavity, skin and lungs are used; respiration by lungs is pulmonary respiration. Lungs are a pair of elongated, pink, sac-like structures in the thorax. During aestivation and hibernation, gas exchange is through the skin.
  • Circulation — well-developed closed vascular system, plus a lymphatic system (lymph, lymph channels, lymph nodes). Heart: three chambers (two atria, one ventricle), covered by the pericardium. A triangular sinus venosus joins the right atrium and receives blood through the vena cava. The ventricle opens into the sac-like conus arteriosus. Arteries form the arterial system; veins the venous system.
  • Portal systems — hepatic portal system (between liver and intestine) and renal portal system (between kidney and lower parts of the body).
  • Blood — plasma and cells: RBCs (erythrocytes, nucleated, with haemoglobin), WBCs (leucocytes) and platelets. Lymph lacks a few proteins and RBCs. Frogs have single circulation.
7.2.2 Frog Anatomy — Excretion, Coordination & Reproduction ▸
  • Excretory system — a pair of kidneys, ureters, cloaca and urinary bladder. Kidneys: compact, dark red, bean-like, posterior in the body cavity on both sides of the vertebral column; made of uriniferous tubules (nephrons). In males the ureters act as a urinogenital duct opening into the cloaca; in females the ureters and oviducts open separately into the cloaca. Thin-walled urinary bladder ventral to the rectum. Frogs excrete urea — ureotelic.
  • Endocrine glands — pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, pineal body, pancreatic islets, adrenals and gonads.
  • Nervous system — central (brain and spinal cord), peripheral (cranial and spinal nerves) and autonomic (sympathetic and parasympathetic). Ten pairs of cranial nerves. Brain enclosed in the brain box (cranium): forebrain (olfactory lobes, paired cerebral hemispheres, unpaired diencephalon), midbrain (a pair of optic lobes), hindbrain (cerebellum and medulla oblongata). The medulla passes out through the foramen magnum into the spinal cord in the vertebral column.
  • Sense organs — touch (sensory papillae), taste (taste buds), smell (nasal epithelium), vision (eyes), hearing (tympanum with internal ears). Eyes and internal ears are well-organised; the rest are cellular aggregations around nerve endings. Eyes are a pair of spherical simple eyes in the orbit. External ear is absent; only the tympanum is seen. The ear serves hearing and balancing (equilibrium).
  • Male reproductive system — a pair of yellowish ovoid testes attached to the upper kidneys by a double fold of peritoneum, the mesorchium. 10–12 vasa efferentia enter the kidneys and open into Bidder's canal, which connects to the urinogenital duct opening into the cloaca. The cloaca is a small median chamber passing faecal matter, urine and sperms.
  • Female reproductive system — a pair of ovaries near the kidneys with no functional connection with them; a pair of oviducts opens separately into the cloaca. A mature female lays 2500–3000 ova at a time.
  • Fertilisation is external, in water; development has a larval stage, the tadpole, which undergoes metamorphosis into the adult.
  • Importance — frogs eat insects and protect crops; maintain ecological balance as a link in food chains and food webs; their muscular legs are eaten in some countries.

Chapter 12 · Respiration in PlantsBoard 2026–27: 6 marks · 1×1m, 1×5mFull notes · current NCERT text
12.1 Respiration & Why Plants Need No Lungs ▸

Respiration is the breaking of C–C bonds of complex compounds through oxidation within cells, releasing a considerable amount of energy. The compounds oxidised are respiratory substrates — usually carbohydrates, but proteins, fats and even organic acids in some plants under certain conditions.

  • Energy is released in a series of slow, step-wise, enzyme-controlled reactions and trapped as ATP, the energy currency of the cell. The carbon skeletons produced are used as precursors for biosynthesis.
  • Complete combustion: C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + Energy. Done in one step, most energy would be lost as heat; the cell oxidises glucose in small steps so the energy can be coupled to ATP synthesis.
  • Do plants breathe? They need O2 and give out CO2, but have no specialised organs — only stomata and lenticels. Three reasons: (1) each plant part takes care of its own gas exchange, with little transport between parts; (2) roots, stems and leaves respire at far lower rates than animals, and during photosynthesis O2 is released inside the cell itself; (3) gases need not diffuse far — living cells lie close to the surface, the interior of woody stems is dead, and loosely packed parenchyma forms an interconnected network of air spaces.
  • Some organisms are facultative anaerobes, others obligate anaerobes; all retain the enzymes to partially oxidise glucose without O2 — glycolysis.
12.2 Glycolysis (EMP Pathway) ▸

From Greek glycos (sugar) + lysis (splitting). Scheme given by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof and J. Parnas — hence the EMP pathway. Occurs in the cytoplasm of all living organisms; in anaerobic organisms it is the only process of respiration. One glucose is partially oxidised to two molecules of pyruvic acid through a chain of ten enzyme-controlled reactions.

  • In plants, glucose comes from sucrose (end product of photosynthesis) or storage carbohydrates. Invertase splits sucrose into glucose + fructose; hexokinase phosphorylates them to glucose-6-phosphate, which isomerises to fructose-6-phosphate.
  • ATP is used at two steps: glucose → glucose-6-phosphate, and fructose-6-phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.
  • Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate splits into dihydroxyacetone phosphate and 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL).
  • NADH + H+ is formed at one step: PGAL → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA).
  • ATP is formed at two steps: BPGA → 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA), and phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) → pyruvic acid.
  • Per glucose: 4 ATP made − 2 ATP used = net 2 ATP, plus 2 NADH + H+.
  • Pyruvic acid has three fates: lactic acid fermentation, alcoholic fermentation or aerobic respiration (Krebs' cycle, needs O2).
12.3 Fermentation ▸
  • Alcoholic fermentation (yeast) — incomplete oxidation of glucose under anaerobic conditions; pyruvic acid → CO2 + ethanol, catalysed by pyruvic acid decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase.
  • Lactic acid fermentation — some bacteria, and our muscle cells during exercise when O2 is inadequate: pyruvic acid is reduced to lactic acid by lactate dehydrogenase.
  • In both, NADH + H+ is the reducing agent and is reoxidised to NAD+.
  • Less than 7% of the energy in glucose is released, and not all of it is trapped as ATP. The processes are hazardous — acid or alcohol is produced. Yeasts poison themselves to death at about 13% alcohol.
  • Fermentation vs aerobic respiration: partial vs complete breakdown of glucose; net 2 ATP vs many more ATP; NADH oxidised to NAD+ slowly vs very vigorously.
12.4 Aerobic Respiration & the Krebs' Cycle ▸

Aerobic respiration is the complete oxidation of organic substances in the presence of O2, releasing CO2, water and a large amount of energy. Pyruvate is transported from the cytoplasm into the mitochondria. Two crucial events: complete oxidation of pyruvate by stepwise removal of all hydrogen atoms, leaving three CO2 (in the matrix); and passing the electrons to O2 with simultaneous ATP synthesis (on the inner membrane).

  • Oxidative decarboxylation (link reaction): Pyruvic acid + CoA + NAD+ → Acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+, catalysed by pyruvate dehydrogenase (needs Mg2+, NAD+ and Coenzyme A). Two NADH per glucose.
  • Krebs' cycle / TCA / citric acid cycle (Hans Krebs): acetyl group + oxaloacetic acid (OAA) + water → citric acid, by citrate synthase (CoA released). Citrate → isocitrate → two decarboxylations → α-ketoglutaric acid → succinyl-CoA → succinic acid → … → OAA regenerated.
  • Succinyl-CoA → succinic acid makes one GTP (substrate-level phosphorylation), which forms ATP from ADP in a coupled reaction.
  • Per turn: 3 NAD+ → NADH + H+ and 1 FAD+ → FADH2.
  • Summary: Pyruvic acid + 4NAD+ + FAD+ + 2H2O + ADP + Pi → 3CO2 + 4NADH + 4H+ + FADH2 + ATP.
  • So far, one glucose has yielded CO2, 8 NADH + H+, 2 FADH2 and just 2 ATP in the TCA cycle.
12.4.2 Electron Transport System & Oxidative Phosphorylation ▸

The electron transport system (ETS) lies in the inner mitochondrial membrane; it releases the energy stored in NADH + H+ and FADH2 by passing their electrons to O2, forming H2O.

  • Complex I — NADH dehydrogenase; passes electrons to ubiquinone. Complex II — brings electrons from FADH2 (succinate oxidation) to ubiquinone.
  • Complex III — cytochrome bc1 complex; reduced ubiquinone (ubiquinol) passes electrons to cytochrome c, a small mobile carrier on the outer surface of the inner membrane, between complexes III and IV.
  • Complex IV — cytochrome c oxidase (cytochromes a and a3, two copper centres). O2 is the final hydrogen acceptor — its role is limited to the terminal stage, yet vital, since it drives the whole process by removing hydrogen.
  • Complex V — ATP synthase: F1 headpiece (peripheral; site of ATP synthesis from ADP + Pi) and F0 (integral; proton channel). For each ATP, 4H+ pass through F0 from the intermembrane space to the matrix.
  • 1 NADH → 3 ATP; 1 FADH2 → 2 ATP.
  • Called oxidative phosphorylation because the energy of oxidation-reduction (not light, as in photophosphorylation) builds the proton gradient. Mechanism: chemiosmotic hypothesis.
12.5–12.7 Balance Sheet, Amphibolic Pathway & RQ ▸
  • Respiratory balance sheet — net gain of 38 ATP per glucose in aerobic respiration, calculated on four assumptions: a sequential orderly pathway (glycolysis → TCA → ETS); NADH from glycolysis enters mitochondria for oxidative phosphorylation; no intermediates are withdrawn for synthesis; only glucose is respired. These are not really valid in a living system, so 38 ATP is a theoretical figure.
  • Points of entry — carbohydrates are first converted to glucose; fats → glycerol (enters as PGAL) + fatty acids (enter as acetyl CoA); proteins → amino acids (after deamination) enter as pyruvate, acetyl CoA or within the Krebs' cycle.
  • Amphibolic pathway — the same compounds are withdrawn from the pathway for synthesis (e.g. acetyl CoA for fatty acid synthesis). Since the respiratory pathway is involved in both catabolism and anabolism, it is called amphibolic rather than catabolic.
  • Respiratory quotient (RQ) = volume of CO2 evolved ÷ volume of O2 consumed. Carbohydrates: 1.0. Fats: less than 1 (tripalmitin: 2C51H98O6 + 145O2 → 102CO2 + 98H2O; RQ = 102/145 = 0.7). Proteins: about 0.9. Pure proteins or fats are never used alone.

13.1 Growth — Indeterminate & Measurable ▸

Development is the sum of two processes: growth and differentiation. The first step of plant growth is seed germination, which occurs only when conditions are favourable; otherwise the seed enters a period of suspended growth (rest).

  • Growth — an irreversible permanent increase in size of an organ, its parts or even a single cell, accompanied by metabolic processes (anabolic and catabolic) at the expense of energy. Expansion of a leaf is growth; swelling of wood in water is not.
  • Indeterminate / open form of growth — plants retain the capacity for unlimited growth throughout life because of meristems, whose cells divide and self-perpetuate. Root and shoot apical meristems cause primary growth (elongation along the axis); in dicots and gymnosperms, the lateral meristems — vascular cambium and cork cambium — cause secondary growth (increase in girth).
  • Growth is measurable — at the cellular level it is an increase in protoplasm, measured indirectly by fresh weight, dry weight, length, area, volume and cell number. A maize root apical meristem gives more than 17,500 new cells per hour (increase in number); watermelon cells may increase in size up to 3,50,000 times (increase in size). Pollen tube growth is measured as length; a dorsiventral leaf as surface area.
13.1.3 Phases of Growth & Growth Rates ▸
  • Meristematic phase — constantly dividing cells at root and shoot apices; rich in protoplasm, large nuclei, thin primary cellulose walls with abundant plasmodesmata.
  • Elongation phase — cells just behind the meristem; increased vacuolation, cell enlargement and new cell-wall deposition. (Detected by the parallel line technique — zones just behind the apex elongate most.)
  • Maturation phase — further away; cells reach maximal size in wall thickening and protoplasmic modification.
  • Growth rate — increased growth per unit time.
  • Arithmetic growth — after mitosis only one daughter cell keeps dividing, the other differentiates; e.g. a root elongating at a constant rate. Plot is a linear curve: Lt = L0 + rt.
  • Geometric growth — both daughter cells keep dividing: slow lag phase → rapid log (exponential) phase → stationary phase as nutrients become limited. Plot is a sigmoid (S) curve, typical of living organisms in a natural environment. W1 = W0ert, where r is the relative growth rate, also called the efficiency index.
  • Absolute growth rate — total growth per unit time. Relative growth rate — growth per unit time per unit initial parameter. (Two leaves gaining the same 5 cm2: the smaller leaf has the higher relative growth rate.)
13.1.5 Conditions for Growth; 13.2 Differentiation; 13.3 Development ▸
  • Conditions — water (cell enlargement and turgidity for extension growth; medium for enzymes), oxygen (releases metabolic energy), nutrients (synthesis of protoplasm and energy source), an optimum temperature range, and environmental signals such as light and gravity.
  • Differentiation — cells from apical meristems and cambium mature to perform specific functions, with structural changes in walls and protoplasm; e.g. a tracheary element loses its protoplasm and develops strong, elastic lignocellulosic secondary walls.
  • Dedifferentiation — living differentiated cells regain the capacity to divide, e.g. formation of interfascicular cambium and cork cambium from fully differentiated parenchyma.
  • Redifferentiation — cells produced by such dedifferentiated tissue again lose the capacity to divide and mature to perform specific functions.
  • Open differentiation — cells from the same meristem have different structures at maturity, depending on position: cells pushed away from the root apical meristem become root-cap cells; those pushed to the periphery become epidermis.
  • Development — all changes in the life cycle from seed germination to senescence; sequence: meristematic cell → plasmatic growth → expansion (elongation) → differentiation → maturation → mature cell → senescence → death.
  • Plasticity — following different pathways in response to environment or life phase to form different structures; e.g. heterophylly in cotton, coriander and larkspur (juvenile vs mature leaves differ), and in buttercup (leaves in air vs in water).
  • Development is controlled by intrinsic factors (genetic; intercellular chemicals such as PGRs) and extrinsic factors (light, temperature, water, oxygen, nutrition).
13.4 Plant Growth Regulators — Characteristics & Discovery ▸

PGRs (plant growth substances, plant hormones, phytohormones) are small, simple molecules of diverse chemistry: indole compounds (IAA), adenine derivatives (kinetin), carotenoid derivatives (ABA), terpenes (GA3) and gases (ethylene, C2H4).

  • Plant growth promoters — auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins (cell division, enlargement, pattern formation, tropic growth, flowering, fruiting, seed formation).
  • Plant growth inhibitors — abscisic acid (responses to wounds and stresses; dormancy, abscission). Ethylene fits either group but is largely an inhibitor.
  • Discovery (each accidental):
    • Auxin — Charles and Francis Darwin saw canary grass coleoptiles bend towards unilateral light (phototropism); the coleoptile tip was the source. Auxin was isolated by F.W. Went from coleoptile tips of oat seedlings.
    • Gibberellin — 'bakanae' (foolish seedling) disease of rice caused by the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi; E. Kurosawa (1926) reproduced the symptoms with sterile fungal filtrates; the active substance was gibberellic acid.
    • Cytokinin — F. Skoog found tobacco callus proliferated only when, besides auxin, the medium had vascular tissue extract, yeast extract, coconut milk or DNA; Miller et al. (1955) crystallised the cytokinesis-promoting substance, kinetin.
    • Abscisic acid — mid-1960s, three inhibitors (inhibitor-B, abscission II, dormin) proved chemically identical — named ABA.
    • Ethylene — H.H. Cousins (1910): a volatile substance from ripened oranges hastened ripening of stored bananas.
13.4.3 Physiological Effects of the Five PGRs ▸
  • Auxins (Greek auxein, to grow; first isolated from human urine) — natural IAA and IBA; synthetic NAA and 2,4-D. Made at growing stem and root apices. Initiate rooting in stem cuttings; promote flowering (pineapple); prevent early fruit/leaf drop but promote abscission of older leaves and fruits; cause apical dominance (apical bud suppresses lateral buds — decapitation releases them, used in tea plantations and hedge-making); induce parthenocarpy (tomato); used as herbicides — 2,4-D kills dicot weeds, not mature monocots (weed-free lawns); control xylem differentiation and help cell division.
  • Gibberellins — over 100 known (GA1, GA2, GA3…); all acidic; GA3 most studied. Increase length of grape stalks; elongate and improve the shape of apples; delay senescence (longer market period); speed up malting in brewing; increase sugarcane stem length and yield by up to 20 tonnes per acre; hasten maturity in juvenile conifers (early seed production); promote bolting (internode elongation just before flowering) in beet, cabbage and rosette plants.
  • Cytokinins — specific effect on cytokinesis; kinetin (modified adenine, a purine) from autoclaved herring sperm DNA — does not occur naturally in plants; natural zeatin from corn kernels and coconut milk. Made where cell division is rapid (root apices, shoot buds, young fruits). Help form new leaves, chloroplasts, lateral shoots and adventitious shoots; overcome apical dominance; promote nutrient mobilisation, delaying leaf senescence.
  • Ethylene — simple gaseous PGR made in large amounts by senescing tissues and ripening fruits. Causes horizontal growth of seedlings, swelling of the axis, apical hook in dicot seedlings; promotes senescence and abscission of leaves and flowers; highly effective in fruit ripening, raising the respiration rate (respiratory climacteric); breaks seed and bud dormancy (peanut germination, potato sprouting); rapid internode elongation in deep-water rice; promotes root growth and root hairs; initiates flowering and synchronises fruit-set in pineapple, induces flowering in mango. Source compound: ethephon — hastens ripening in tomato and apple, thins flowers/fruits (cotton, cherry, walnut), promotes female flowers in cucumber.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA) — general growth and metabolism inhibitor; inhibits seed germination; stimulates stomatal closure; increases stress tolerance — hence the stress hormone; important in seed development, maturation and dormancy (helps seeds withstand desiccation). Usually an antagonist to GAs.
  • PGR roles may be complementary or antagonistic, individualistic or synergistic; several PGRs interact in events like dormancy, abscission, senescence and apical dominance.

14.1 Respiratory Organs & the Human Respiratory System ▸

Breathing (commonly called respiration) is the exchange of O2 from the atmosphere with CO2 produced by the cells.

  • In animals — simple diffusion over the body surface (sponges, coelenterates, flatworms); moist cuticle (earthworm); tracheal tubes (insects); gills — branchial respiration (most aquatic arthropods and molluscs, fishes); lungs — pulmonary respiration (amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals). Frogs also use moist skin — cutaneous respiration.
  • Human pathway — external nostrils → nasal passage → nasal chamber → pharynx (common passage for food and air) → larynx (cartilaginous sound box) → trachea → divides at the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left primary bronchi → secondary, tertiary bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → alveoli (thin, irregular-walled, vascularised bags).
  • Epiglottis — thin elastic cartilaginous flap that covers the glottis during swallowing, preventing food from entering the larynx.
  • Trachea, primary/secondary/tertiary bronchi and initial bronchioles are supported by incomplete cartilaginous rings.
  • Two lungs covered by a double-layered pleura with pleural fluid between, reducing friction. Outer pleura touches the thoracic lining; inner pleura touches the lung surface.
  • Conducting part (nostrils to terminal bronchioles) — transports air, clears foreign particles, humidifies it and brings it to body temperature. Respiratory / exchange part (alveoli and their ducts) — site of actual diffusion of O2 and CO2.
  • The thoracic chamber is air-tight: vertebral column (dorsal), sternum (ventral), ribs (lateral), dome-shaped diaphragm (below). Any change in thoracic volume is reflected in lung volume.
  • Steps of respiration: (i) breathing / pulmonary ventilation; (ii) diffusion of gases across the alveolar membrane; (iii) transport of gases by blood; (iv) diffusion between blood and tissues; (v) utilisation of O2 by cells (cellular respiration).
14.2 Mechanism of Breathing & Respiratory Volumes ▸

Air moves because of a pressure gradient between lungs and atmosphere, created by the diaphragm and the external and internal intercostal muscles.

  • Inspiration — diaphragm contracts (volume increases in the antero-posterior axis); external intercostals contract, lifting ribs and sternum (volume increases in the dorso-ventral axis). Pulmonary volume rises, intra-pulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric, and air rushes in.
  • Expiration — diaphragm and intercostals relax; thoracic and pulmonary volumes fall; intra-pulmonary pressure rises slightly above atmospheric; air is expelled. Abdominal muscles can add strength to both.
  • A healthy human breathes 12–16 times per minute. Volumes are measured with a spirometer.
  • Tidal volume (TV) — air inspired or expired in normal respiration, about 500 mL (6000–8000 mL per minute).
  • Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) — extra air by forcible inspiration, 2500–3000 mL. Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) — extra air by forcible expiration, 1000–1100 mL. Residual volume (RV) — air left after forcible expiration, 1100–1200 mL.
  • Capacities: Inspiratory capacity (IC) = TV + IRV; Expiratory capacity (EC) = TV + ERV; Functional residual capacity (FRC) = ERV + RV; Vital capacity (VC) = ERV + TV + IRV — maximum air breathed in after a forced expiration (or out after a forced inspiration); Total lung capacity (TLC) = RV + ERV + TV + IRV = VC + RV.
14.3 Exchange of Gases ▸
  • Alveoli are the primary sites of exchange; exchange also occurs between blood and tissues — by simple diffusion along pressure/concentration gradients. Solubility of gases and thickness of membranes also affect the rate.
  • Partial pressure — pressure contributed by one gas in a mixture (pO2, pCO2). In mm Hg (Table 14.1): O2 — atmosphere 159, alveoli 104, deoxygenated blood 40, oxygenated blood 95, tissues 40. CO2 — atmosphere 0.3, alveoli 40, deoxygenated blood 45, oxygenated blood 40, tissues 45.
  • So O2 moves alveoli → blood → tissues; CO2 moves tissues → blood → alveoli. CO2 is 20–25 times more soluble than O2, so it diffuses much more per unit pressure difference.
  • Diffusion membrane — three layers: thin squamous epithelium of alveoli, endothelium of alveolar capillaries, and basement substance between them; total thickness much less than a millimetre.
14.4 Transport of Gases ▸
  • O2: about 97% carried by RBCs, 3% dissolved in plasma. CO2: 20–25% by RBCs, 70% as bicarbonate, about 7% dissolved in plasma.
  • Haemoglobin — red, iron-containing pigment in RBCs; binds O2 reversibly as oxyhaemoglobin; each molecule carries up to four O2. Binding depends mainly on pO2; pCO2, H+ concentration and temperature interfere.
  • Oxygen dissociation curve — percentage saturation of haemoglobin plotted against pO2 gives a sigmoid curve. In alveoli (high pO2, low pCO2, less H+, lower temperature) oxyhaemoglobin forms; in tissues (low pO2, high pCO2, high H+, higher temperature) O2 dissociates. Every 100 mL of oxygenated blood delivers about 5 mL O2 to tissues.
  • Carbamino-haemoglobin — CO2 bound to haemoglobin (20–25%); binding favoured by high pCO2 and low pO2 (tissues), released at alveoli.
  • Carbonic anhydrase (very high in RBCs, minute in plasma): CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ HCO3− + H+. At tissues CO2 forms HCO3−; at alveoli the reaction reverses, releasing CO2. Every 100 mL of deoxygenated blood delivers about 4 mL CO2 to alveoli.
14.5 Regulation & 14.6 Disorders ▸
  • Respiratory rhythm centre in the medulla — primarily responsible for regulation.
  • Pneumotaxic centre in the pons — moderates the rhythm centre; its signals reduce the duration of inspiration and so alter the respiratory rate.
  • Chemosensitive area next to the rhythm centre — highly sensitive to CO2 and H+; receptors on the aortic arch and carotid artery also detect them. The role of O2 in regulating the rhythm is quite insignificant.
  • Asthma — difficulty in breathing with wheezing, due to inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles.
  • Emphysema — chronic disorder: alveolar walls damaged, respiratory surface decreased; a major cause is cigarette smoking.
  • Occupational respiratory disorders — dust from grinding or stone-breaking overwhelms body defences; long exposure causes inflammation and fibrosis (proliferation of fibrous tissue), seriously damaging lungs. Workers should wear protective masks.

15.1 Blood — Plasma & Formed Elements ▸

Blood is a special connective tissue: a fluid matrix (plasma) plus formed elements. (Sponges and coelenterates simply circulate surrounding water through body cavities.)

  • Plasma (~55%) — straw-coloured, viscous; 90–92% water, 6–8% proteins: fibrinogen (clotting), globulins (defence), albumins (osmotic balance). Also minerals (Na+, Ca++, Mg++, HCO3−, Cl−), glucose, amino acids, lipids, and inactive clotting factors. Serum = plasma without clotting factors.
  • Formed elements (~45%) — erythrocytes, leucocytes and platelets.
  • RBCs (erythrocytes) — most abundant; 5–5.5 million mm−3; formed in red bone marrow; no nucleus in most mammals; biconcave; contain haemoglobin (12–16 g per 100 mL blood); life span 120 days, destroyed in the spleen (graveyard of RBCs).
  • WBCs (leucocytes) — colourless, nucleated, 6000–8000 mm−3, short-lived. Granulocytes: neutrophils (most abundant, 60–65%, phagocytic), eosinophils (2–3%, resist infections, allergic reactions), basophils (least, 0.5–1%, secrete histamine, serotonin, heparin — inflammation). Agranulocytes: lymphocytes (20–25%, B and T types — immune responses), monocytes (6–8%, phagocytic).
  • Platelets (thrombocytes) — cell fragments from megakaryocytes in bone marrow; 1,50,000–3,50,000 mm−3; release substances for clotting — a reduction causes clotting disorders and excessive blood loss.
15.1.3 Blood Groups & 15.1.4 Coagulation ▸
  • ABO grouping — based on antigens A and B on RBCs and natural antibodies in plasma. Group A: antigen A, anti-B, receives A, O. Group B: antigen B, anti-A, receives B, O. Group AB: A and B, no antibodies, receives AB, A, B, O. Group O: no antigen, anti-A and anti-B, receives only O. O = universal donor; AB = universal recipient. Mismatched transfusion causes clumping (destruction of RBCs).
  • Rh grouping — Rh antigen (as in Rhesus monkeys) on RBCs of nearly 80% of humans (Rh+ve); absent in Rh−ve. An Rh−ve person exposed to Rh+ve blood forms anti-Rh antibodies, so Rh must also be matched.
  • Erythroblastosis foetalis — Rh−ve mother, Rh+ve foetus. No exposure in the first pregnancy (placenta separates the bloods), but during delivery the mother may be exposed and start making antibodies. In later pregnancies these antibodies leak into foetal blood and destroy foetal RBCs — fatal, or severe anaemia and jaundice. Prevented by giving anti-Rh antibodies to the mother immediately after the first delivery.
  • Coagulation — a clot (coagulum) is a network of fibrin threads trapping dead and damaged formed elements. Injury stimulates platelets (and tissue factors) to start a cascade producing thrombokinase; thrombokinase converts inactive prothrombin → thrombin; thrombin converts fibrinogen → fibrin. Calcium ions play a very important role.
15.2 Lymph & 15.3 Circulatory Pathways ▸
  • Interstitial / tissue fluid — water and small solutes that leave capillaries into spaces between cells (larger proteins and most formed elements stay behind); same mineral distribution as plasma; all exchange between blood and cells occurs through it.
  • Lymph — the fluid collected by the lymphatic system and drained back into major veins; colourless, contains specialised lymphocytes (immunity); carries nutrients and hormones; fats are absorbed through lymph in the lacteals of intestinal villi.
  • Open circulation (arthropods, molluscs) — blood pumped into open spaces called sinuses. Closed circulation (annelids, chordates) — blood always in a closed network of vessels; more advantageous, as flow can be precisely regulated.
  • Heart in vertebrates — fishes: 2-chambered, pumps deoxygenated blood (single circulation); amphibians and reptiles (except crocodiles): 3-chambered, blood mixes in the single ventricle (incomplete double circulation); crocodiles, birds, mammals: 4-chambered, no mixing (double circulation).
15.3.1 The Human Heart ▸
  • Mesodermally derived; in the thoracic cavity between the lungs, slightly tilted left; size of a clenched fist; enclosed in the double-walled pericardium with pericardial fluid.
  • Two small upper atria and two larger lower ventricles. Thin inter-atrial septum, thick inter-ventricular septum, and a thick fibrous atrio-ventricular septum between atrium and ventricle of the same side.
  • Valves — tricuspid (three cusps) between right atrium and right ventricle; bicuspid / mitral between left atrium and left ventricle; semilunar valves at the openings of the pulmonary artery and aorta. They allow one-way flow only.
  • The whole heart is cardiac muscle; ventricle walls are much thicker than atrial walls.
  • Nodal tissue — SAN (right upper corner of right atrium); AVN (lower left corner of right atrium near the AV septum); AV bundle (bundle of His) runs through the AV septum, divides into right and left bundles giving Purkinje fibres. Nodal tissue is autoexcitable.
  • SAN generates the most action potentials (70–75 min−1), initiating and maintaining rhythm — the pacemaker. Normal heart rate 70–75 (average 72) beats per minute.
15.3.2 Cardiac Cycle & 15.3.3 ECG ▸
  • Joint diastole — all chambers relaxed; tricuspid and bicuspid valves open, blood flows from pulmonary veins and vena cava through atria into ventricles; semilunar valves closed.
  • Atrial systole — SAN fires; both atria contract, increasing ventricular filling by about 30%.
  • Ventricular systole — impulse passes via AVN, AV bundle and bundle of His; ventricles contract while atria relax. Rising pressure closes tricuspid and bicuspid valves, then forces semilunar valves open, sending blood into the pulmonary artery and aorta.
  • Ventricular diastole — pressure falls, semilunar valves close; AV valves reopen; back to joint diastole.
  • 72 cycles per minute, so one cardiac cycle = 0.8 s. Stroke volume ≈ 70 mL per ventricle per cycle. Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate ≈ 5000 mL (5 L) per minute; much higher in athletes.
  • Heart sounds — first sound 'lub': closure of tricuspid and bicuspid valves; second sound 'dub': closure of semilunar valves.
  • ECG — graphical record of the heart's electrical activity; standard ECG uses three leads (each wrist and the left ankle). P-wave: depolarisation of atria (atrial contraction). QRS complex: depolarisation of ventricles (contraction starts shortly after Q, marking the start of systole). T-wave: repolarisation of ventricles (end of T marks the end of systole). Counting QRS complexes gives the heart rate; deviations indicate disease.
15.4 Double Circulation; 15.5 Regulation; 15.6 Disorders ▸
  • Blood vessels have three layers: tunica intima (squamous endothelium), tunica media (smooth muscle and elastic fibres — thinner in veins), tunica externa (fibrous connective tissue with collagen).
  • Pulmonary circulation — right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium. Systemic circulation — left ventricle → aorta → arteries, arterioles, capillaries → tissues → venules, veins, vena cava → right atrium.
  • Hepatic portal system — hepatic portal vein carries blood from intestine to liver before the systemic circulation. Coronary system — vessels exclusively supplying the cardiac muscle.
  • Regulation — the heart is myogenic (auto-regulated by nodal tissue). A neural centre in the medulla oblongata moderates it via the ANS: sympathetic nerves increase heart rate, strength of ventricular contraction and cardiac output; parasympathetic signals decrease heart rate, conduction speed and cardiac output. Adrenal medullary hormones also increase cardiac output.
  • Hypertension — BP above normal 120/80 mm Hg (systolic/diastolic); repeated readings of 140/90 or higher; leads to heart disease and affects brain and kidney.
  • Coronary artery disease (atherosclerosis) — deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol and fibrous tissue narrow the lumen of arteries supplying the heart muscle.
  • Angina (angina pectoris) — acute chest pain when not enough O2 reaches heart muscle; more common in middle-aged and elderly.
  • Heart failure — heart not pumping effectively enough; also called congestive heart failure (lung congestion). Not the same as cardiac arrest (heart stops) or heart attack (heart muscle suddenly damaged by inadequate blood supply).

16.0 Nitrogenous Wastes & Excretory Structures ▸

Animals accumulate ammonia, urea, uric acid, CO2, water and ions (Na+, K+, Cl−, phosphate, sulphate). Ammonia is the most toxic and needs a lot of water; uric acid is the least toxic and is removed with minimum water loss.

  • Ammonotelism — excreting ammonia: many bony fishes, aquatic amphibians, aquatic insects. Being readily soluble, it leaves by diffusion across body or gill surfaces as ammonium ions; kidneys play no significant role.
  • Ureotelism — excreting urea: mammals, many terrestrial amphibians, marine fishes. Ammonia is converted to urea in the liver, filtered out by the kidneys; some urea may be retained in the kidney matrix to maintain osmolarity.
  • Uricotelism — excreting uric acid as pellet or paste: reptiles, birds, land snails, insects. Terrestrial life favoured less toxic urea and uric acid to conserve water.
  • Excretory structures — protonephridia / flame cells: Platyhelminthes (Planaria), rotifers, some annelids and the cephalochordate Amphioxus (mainly osmoregulation). Nephridia: earthworms and other annelids. Malpighian tubules: most insects including cockroaches. Antennal / green glands: crustaceans like prawns. Vertebrates have complex tubular kidneys.
16.1 Human Excretory System & the Nephron ▸
  • A pair of kidneys, a pair of ureters, a urinary bladder and a urethra.
  • Kidneys — reddish-brown, bean-shaped, between the last thoracic and third lumbar vertebrae near the dorsal inner wall of the abdominal cavity; each 10–12 cm long, 5–7 cm wide, 2–3 cm thick, 120–170 g.
  • Hilum — notch on the inner concave surface where ureter, blood vessels and nerves enter. Inside it, the funnel-shaped renal pelvis with projections called calyces. Outer tough capsule; outer cortex and inner medulla. Medulla forms conical medullary pyramids projecting into calyces; cortex extends between them as columns of Bertini.
  • Each kidney has nearly one million nephrons — the functional units. Each has a glomerulus (tuft of capillaries from the afferent arteriole, a branch of the renal artery; blood leaves by the efferent arteriole) and a renal tubule.
  • Tubule: double-walled cup-like Bowman's capsule (encloses glomerulus — together the Malpighian body / renal corpuscle) → PCT → hairpin Henle's loop (descending and ascending limbs) → DCT → collecting duct (DCTs of many nephrons join; collecting ducts open into the renal pelvis through the medullary pyramids).
  • Malpighian corpuscle, PCT and DCT lie in the cortex; Henle's loop dips into the medulla. Cortical nephrons (majority) — short loop; juxtamedullary nephrons — long loop deep into the medulla.
  • The efferent arteriole forms peritubular capillaries around the tubule; a minute vessel running parallel to Henle's loop forms the U-shaped vasa recta (absent or highly reduced in cortical nephrons).
16.2 Urine Formation ▸

Three processes: glomerular filtration, reabsorption and secretion.

  • Glomerular filtration — kidneys filter 1100–1200 mL of blood per minute (about 1/5 of each ventricle's output). Glomerular capillary blood pressure filters blood through three layers: endothelium of glomerular vessels, epithelium of Bowman's capsule, and a basement membrane between. Bowman's capsule epithelial cells, podocytes, leave minute filtration slits (slit pores). Almost all plasma constituents except proteins pass through — ultrafiltration.
  • GFR — filtrate formed per minute: about 125 mL/min, i.e. 180 L per day.
  • JGA (juxtaglomerular apparatus) — sensitive region formed by cellular modifications of the DCT and afferent arteriole where they touch. A fall in GFR activates JG cells to release renin, restoring glomerular blood flow and GFR.
  • Reabsorption — 180 L filtrate vs 1.5 L urine, so nearly 99% is reabsorbed. Glucose, amino acids, Na+ are reabsorbed actively; nitrogenous wastes passively; water passively in the initial segments.
  • Tubular secretion — tubular cells secrete H+, K+ and ammonia into the filtrate, maintaining ionic and acid-base balance.
16.3 Function of the Tubules & 16.4 Counter-current Mechanism ▸
  • PCT — simple cuboidal brush-border epithelium (more surface area). Reabsorbs nearly all essential nutrients and 70–80% of electrolytes and water; maintains pH and ionic balance by secreting H+ and ammonia and absorbing HCO3−.
  • Henle's loop — reabsorption is minimum in the ascending limb, but it maintains high osmolarity of medullary interstitial fluid. Descending limb: permeable to water, almost impermeable to electrolytes (filtrate concentrates going down). Ascending limb: impermeable to water, allows transport of electrolytes (filtrate gets diluted going up).
  • DCT — conditional reabsorption of Na+ and water; reabsorbs HCO3−; selectively secretes H+, K+ and NH3, maintaining pH and Na–K balance.
  • Collecting duct — from cortex to inner medulla; large amounts of water reabsorbed to make concentrated urine; lets small amounts of urea into the medullary interstitium to keep osmolarity; secretes H+ and K+.
  • Counter-current mechanism — filtrate flows in opposite directions in the two limbs of Henle's loop, and blood in the two limbs of the vasa recta, both in counter-current. This maintains an increasing osmolarity towards the inner medulla, from 300 mOsmol L−1 in the cortex to about 1200 mOsmol L−1 in the inner medulla, caused mainly by NaCl and urea. NaCl from the ascending limb of Henle's loop is exchanged with the descending limb of vasa recta and returned by its ascending limb; urea entering the thin ascending limb is returned to the interstitium by the collecting tubule. The gradient lets water leave the collecting tubule easily; human kidneys make urine nearly four times as concentrated as the initial filtrate.
16.5 Regulation of Kidney Function & 16.6 Micturition ▸
  • Regulated by hormonal feedback involving the hypothalamus, JGA and, to an extent, the heart.
  • ADH (vasopressin) — excessive fluid loss activates osmoreceptors, which stimulate the hypothalamus to release ADH from the neurohypophysis. ADH increases water reabsorption from later parts of the tubule, preventing diuresis; increased body fluid switches osmoreceptors off. ADH also constricts blood vessels, raising blood pressure and GFR.
  • Renin-angiotensin mechanism — a fall in glomerular blood flow/pressure/GFR makes JG cells release renin: angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → angiotensin II. Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor (raises glomerular pressure and GFR) and activates the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone, which causes reabsorption of Na+ and water from distal parts of the tubule.
  • ANF (atrial natriuretic factor) — released when blood flow to the atria increases; causes vasodilation, lowering blood pressure — a check on the renin-angiotensin mechanism.
  • Micturition — urine is stored in the bladder; stretching of its walls activates stretch receptors that signal the CNS; the CNS sends motor messages to contract the bladder's smooth muscles and relax the urethral sphincter. The neural mechanism is the micturition reflex.
  • An adult excretes 1–1.5 L of urine per day — light yellow, slightly acidic (pH 6.0), characteristic odour; 25–30 g of urea per day. Glucose (glycosuria) and ketone bodies (ketonuria) in urine indicate diabetes mellitus.
16.7 Other Organs in Excretion & 16.8 Disorders ▸
  • Lungs — remove large amounts of CO2 (about 200 mL/min) and significant water. Liver (largest gland) — secretes bile containing bilirubin, biliverdin, cholesterol, degraded steroid hormones, vitamins and drugs, passed out with digestive wastes.
  • Skin — sweat glands: watery sweat with NaCl, small amounts of urea and lactic acid (mainly for cooling). Sebaceous glands: sterols, hydrocarbons and waxes through sebum (protective oily covering). Small amounts of nitrogenous wastes leave through saliva.
  • Uremia — accumulation of urea in blood from kidney malfunction; may lead to kidney failure.
  • Hemodialysis — blood from a convenient artery, with anticoagulant heparin added, is pumped into a dialysing unit (artificial kidney): a coiled porous cellophane tube in dialysing fluid of the same composition as plasma except nitrogenous wastes, so wastes move out by concentration gradient. Cleared blood returns through a vein after adding anti-heparin.
  • Kidney transplantation — the ultimate correction for acute renal failure; donor preferably a close relative to minimise immune rejection.
  • Renal calculi — stones of crystallised salts (oxalates etc.) in the kidney. Glomerulonephritis — inflammation of the glomeruli.

17.1 Movement, Locomotion & Types of Movement ▸
  • Movement is a significant feature of living beings — protoplasmic streaming (Amoeba), cilia, flagella, tentacles. Voluntary movements that change place or location are locomotion (walking, running, climbing, flying, swimming). All locomotions are movements, but all movements are not locomotions.
  • The same structures may serve both: Paramoecium cilia move food through the cytopharynx and help locomotion; Hydra tentacles capture prey and help locomotion. Locomotion is for food, shelter, mate, breeding grounds, favourable climate, or escape from predators.
  • Amoeboid movement — macrophages and leucocytes; by pseudopodia from protoplasmic streaming; microfilaments also involved.
  • Ciliary movement — internal tubular organs lined by ciliated epithelium; cilia in the trachea remove dust and inhaled foreign particles; cilia help move ova through the female reproductive tract.
  • Muscular movement — limbs, jaws, tongue; locomotion needs coordinated muscular, skeletal and neural activity. (Flagellar movement: swimming of sperms, water current in sponge canal system, locomotion of Euglena.)
17.2 Muscle — Types & Structure of Skeletal Muscle ▸

Muscle is a specialised tissue of mesodermal origin; 40–50% of adult body weight. Properties: excitability, contractility, extensibility and elasticity.

  • Skeletal — attached to skeletal components; striped (striated); voluntary; locomotion and posture.
  • Visceral — inner walls of hollow organs (alimentary canal, reproductive tract); no striations (smooth, non-striated); involuntary; move food and gametes.
  • Cardiac — heart muscle; cells assembled in a branching pattern; striated but involuntary.
  • A skeletal muscle is made of fascicles (muscle bundles) held by collagenous connective tissue, the fascia; each fascicle contains muscle fibres.
  • Each muscle fibre is lined by the sarcolemma enclosing sarcoplasm; it is a syncytium (many nuclei). Its ER, the sarcoplasmic reticulum, is the store house of Ca++. It contains many parallel myofibrils (myofilaments).
  • Striations come from actin and myosin: light I-band (isotropic) has actin (thin filaments); dark A-band (anisotropic) has myosin (thick filaments). An elastic Z line bisects each I band, holding thin filaments; thick filaments are held at the middle of the A band by the M line.
  • Sarcomere — portion of myofibril between two successive Z lines; the functional unit of contraction. At rest, thin filaments partly overlap thick filaments, leaving the central H zone not overlapped.
17.2.1 Contractile Proteins & 17.2.2 Mechanism of Contraction ▸
  • Actin (thin) filament — two F (filamentous) actins helically wound, each a polymer of monomeric G (globular) actins; two filaments of tropomyosin run alongside; troponin lies at regular intervals on tropomyosin. At rest, a troponin subunit masks the active binding sites for myosin on actin.
  • Myosin (thick) filament — polymer of monomers called meromyosins, each with a globular head and short arm (heavy meromyosin, HMM) and a tail (light meromyosin, LMM). The head and short arm project outwards as the cross arm. The head is an active ATPase with ATP binding sites and active sites for actin.
  • Sliding filament theory — contraction occurs by sliding of thin filaments over thick filaments.
  • Steps: CNS signal via a motor neuron (motor neuron + its muscle fibres = motor unit) reaches the neuromuscular junction / motor-end plate → acetylcholine released → action potential in sarcolemma → Ca++ released into sarcoplasm → Ca++ binds troponin, unmasking active sites on actin → using energy from ATP hydrolysis, myosin head binds actin, forming a cross bridge → actin is pulled towards the centre of the A band, Z lines pulled inwards → sarcomere shortens.
  • During contraction the I bands get reduced while the A bands retain their length (H zone also shortens). Myosin releases ADP and Pi and relaxes; a new ATP binds and breaks the cross bridge; ATP is hydrolysed again and the cycle repeats.
  • Relaxation — Ca++ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic cisternae, actin is masked again and Z lines return.
  • Fatigue — repeated activation accumulates lactic acid from anaerobic breakdown of glycogen.
  • Red fibres — high myoglobin (red O2-storing pigment), plenty of mitochondria — aerobic muscles. White fibres — little myoglobin, few mitochondria, high sarcoplasmic reticulum — depend on anaerobic processes.
17.3 Skeletal System ▸

Bones (hard matrix of calcium salts) and a few cartilages (pliable matrix of chondroitin salts). Human skeleton: 206 bones — axial (80) and appendicular.

  • Skull — 22 bones: 8 cranial (form the cranium) and 14 facial. A U-shaped hyoid at the base of the buccal cavity. Each middle ear has three ear ossicles: malleus, incus, stapes. The skull articulates with the vertebral column by two occipital condyles — dicondylic skull.
  • Vertebral column — 26 vertebrae, dorsal; each has a neural canal for the spinal cord. First vertebra = atlas (articulates with occipital condyles). Regions: cervical 7, thoracic 12, lumbar 5, sacral 1 (fused), coccygeal 1 (fused). Almost all mammals have 7 cervical vertebrae. Protects spinal cord, supports head, attachment for ribs and back muscles.
  • Sternum — flat bone on the ventral midline of the thorax.
  • Ribs — 12 pairs; each thin, flat, with two articulation surfaces dorsally — bicephalic. True ribs (1st–7th): attached dorsally to thoracic vertebrae and ventrally to the sternum by hyaline cartilage. Vertebrochondral (false) ribs (8th, 9th, 10th): join the 7th rib through hyaline cartilage. Floating ribs (11th, 12th): not connected ventrally. Thoracic vertebrae + ribs + sternum = rib cage.
  • Appendicular skeleton — limb bones + girdles; each limb has 30 bones. Fore limb: humerus, radius, ulna, carpals (8), metacarpals (5), phalanges (14). Hind limb: femur (thigh bone, longest), tibia, fibula, tarsals (7), metatarsals (5), phalanges (14); cup-shaped patella (knee cap).
  • Pectoral girdle — each half has a clavicle (collar bone, long, two curvatures) and a scapula (large triangular flat bone between the 2nd and 7th ribs dorsally; ridge called the spine ends as the acromion, which articulates with the clavicle; the glenoid cavity below it articulates with the humerus head — shoulder joint).
  • Pelvic girdle — two coxal bones, each from fused ilium, ischium and pubis; at the fusion point the acetabulum receives the thigh bone; the halves meet ventrally at the pubic symphysis (fibrous cartilage).
17.4 Joints & 17.5 Disorders ▸
  • Joints are points of contact between bones, or bone and cartilage; the joint acts as a fulcrum.
  • Fibrous joints — no movement; flat skull bones fused by sutures of dense fibrous tissue (cranium).
  • Cartilaginous joints — bones joined by cartilage; limited movement; e.g. between adjacent vertebrae.
  • Synovial joints — fluid-filled synovial cavity between articulating surfaces; considerable movement. Ball and socket (humerus and pectoral girdle), hinge (knee), pivot (atlas and axis), gliding (between carpals), saddle (carpal and metacarpal of thumb).
  • Myasthenia gravis — autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction: fatigue, weakening, paralysis of skeletal muscle.
  • Muscular dystrophy — progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle, mostly genetic.
  • Tetany — rapid spasms (wild contractions) due to low Ca++ in body fluid.
  • Arthritis — inflammation of joints. Gout — inflammation of joints due to uric acid crystals.
  • Osteoporosis — age-related decrease in bone mass, more fractures; decreased estrogen is a common cause.

18.1 Neural System & 18.2 Human Neural System ▸

Coordination is the process through which two or more organs interact and complement each other's functions (e.g. during exercise, respiration, heart beat and blood flow all increase). The neural system gives quick, point-to-point coordination; the endocrine system gives chemical integration through hormones.

  • Neural systems are made of neurons, which detect, receive and transmit stimuli. Simple network of neurons in Hydra; brain plus ganglia in insects; most developed in vertebrates.
  • Central neural system (CNS) — brain and spinal cord; site of information processing and control.
  • Peripheral neural system (PNS) — all nerves associated with the CNS. Nerve fibres are afferent (tissues/organs → CNS) or efferent (CNS → peripheral tissues/organs).
  • PNS divisions: somatic neural system (CNS → skeletal muscles) and autonomic neural system (CNS → involuntary organs and smooth muscles), the latter split into sympathetic and parasympathetic systems.
  • Visceral nervous system — part of the PNS: the whole complex of nerves, fibres, ganglia and plexuses carrying impulses between the CNS and the viscera.
18.3 The Neuron ▸
  • Three parts: cell body (cytoplasm, organelles and Nissl's granules), dendrites (short, repeatedly branched fibres with Nissl's granules; carry impulses towards the cell body) and axon (long fibre, branched at the distal end; carries impulses away from the cell body to a synapse or neuromuscular junction).
  • Each axon branch ends in a bulb-like synaptic knob with synaptic vesicles containing neurotransmitters.
  • Multipolar — one axon, two or more dendrites (cerebral cortex). Bipolar — one axon, one dendrite (retina). Unipolar — cell body with one axon only (usually embryonic stage).
  • Myelinated fibres — enveloped by Schwann cells forming a myelin sheath; the gaps between adjacent sheaths are nodes of Ranvier; found in spinal and cranial nerves. Non-myelinated fibres — Schwann cell does not form a myelin sheath; common in the autonomous and somatic neural systems.
18.3.1 Generation & Conduction of the Nerve Impulse ▸
  • Neurons are excitable because their membranes are polarised. At rest, the axonal membrane is more permeable to K+, nearly impermeable to Na+, and impermeable to negatively charged proteins in the axoplasm.
  • So the axoplasm has high K+ and negative proteins, low Na+; the outside fluid has low K+, high Na+. These gradients are maintained by the sodium-potassium pump: 3 Na+ out for 2 K+ in.
  • Outer surface positive, inner surface negative — polarised. The potential difference across the resting membrane is the resting potential.
  • Depolarisation — a stimulus at site A makes the membrane freely permeable to Na+; rapid influx of Na+ reverses polarity (outside negative, inside positive). The potential difference at site A is the action potential = nerve impulse.
  • Conduction — current flows on the inner surface from A to the next site B (and B to A on the outer surface), reversing polarity and generating an action potential at B; repeated along the axon.
  • Repolarisation — the rise in Na+ permeability is extremely short-lived and quickly followed by a rise in K+ permeability; K+ diffuses out, restoring the resting potential, and the fibre becomes responsive again.
18.3.2 Transmission of Impulses — Synapses ▸
  • A synapse is formed by membranes of a pre-synaptic and a post-synaptic neuron, which may or may not be separated by a gap, the synaptic cleft.
  • Electrical synapse — membranes in very close proximity; current flows directly from one neuron to the other; similar to conduction along an axon; always faster than a chemical synapse; rare in our system.
  • Chemical synapse — membranes separated by a fluid-filled synaptic cleft. An arriving action potential moves synaptic vesicles to the membrane; they fuse and release neurotransmitters into the cleft; these bind specific receptors on the post-synaptic membrane, opening ion channels; the new potential may be excitatory or inhibitory.
18.4 Central Neural System — The Brain ▸

The brain is the 'command and control system': voluntary movements, balance, vital involuntary organs (lungs, heart, kidneys), thermoregulation, hunger and thirst, circadian (24-hour) rhythms, endocrine glands and behaviour; also processing of vision, hearing, speech, memory, intelligence, emotions and thoughts.

  • Protected by the skull and covered by cranial meninges: outer dura mater, thin middle arachnoid, inner pia mater (in contact with brain tissue).
  • Forebrain — cerebrum, thalamus, hypothalamus.
    • Cerebrum — major part; a deep cleft divides it into left and right cerebral hemispheres, connected by the corpus callosum. The folded cerebral cortex is grey matter (neuron cell bodies), with motor areas, sensory areas and association areas (intersensory associations, memory, communication). Myelinated fibre tracts inside form the white matter.
    • Thalamus — wrapped by the cerebrum; major coordinating centre for sensory and motor signalling.
    • Hypothalamus — at the base of the thalamus; centres controlling body temperature and the urge to eat and drink; neurosecretory cells secrete hypothalamic hormones.
    • Limbic system — inner parts of the hemispheres with deep structures like the amygdala and hippocampus; with the hypothalamus, regulates sexual behaviour, emotional reactions (excitement, pleasure, rage, fear) and motivation.
  • Midbrain — between thalamus/hypothalamus and pons; the cerebral aqueduct passes through it; dorsal part has four round swellings, the corpora quadrigemina; receives and integrates visual, tactile and auditory inputs.
  • Hindbrain — pons (fibre tracts interconnecting brain regions), cerebellum (very convoluted surface for more neurons; integrates information from the semicircular canals of the ear and the auditory system) and medulla oblongata (connected to the spinal cord; centres for respiration, cardiovascular reflexes and gastric secretions).
  • Brain stem — midbrain, pons and medulla oblongata; connects brain and spinal cord.

19.1 Endocrine Glands & Hormones ▸
  • Neural coordination is fast but short-lived, and nerves do not reach every cell, so continuous regulation is done by hormones. The neural and endocrine systems jointly coordinate the body.
  • Endocrine glands lack ducts — ductless glands; their secretions are hormones.
  • Hormones (current definition) — non-nutrient chemicals which act as intercellular messengers and are produced in trace amounts. Invertebrates have simple endocrine systems; vertebrates use a large number of hormones.
  • Organised endocrine bodies: pituitary, pineal, thyroid, adrenal, pancreas, parathyroid, thymus and gonads (testis, ovary). Gastrointestinal tract, liver, kidney and heart also produce hormones.
19.2.1 Hypothalamus & 19.2.2 Pituitary ▸
  • Hypothalamus — basal part of the diencephalon (forebrain); neurosecretory cells called nuclei make hormones that regulate pituitary hormones: releasing hormones (e.g. GnRH stimulates release of gonadotrophins) and inhibiting hormones (e.g. somatostatin inhibits growth hormone release). They pass down axons and reach the anterior pituitary through a portal circulatory system; the posterior pituitary is under direct neural regulation.
  • Pituitary — in a bony cavity, the sella tursica, attached to the hypothalamus by a stalk. Adenohypophysis = pars distalis (anterior pituitary) + pars intermedia; neurohypophysis = pars nervosa (posterior pituitary).
  • Pars distalis (six hormones): GH — excess: gigantism; deficiency: pituitary dwarfism; excess in adults: acromegaly (disfigurement, especially of face). Prolactin (PRL) — growth of mammary glands and milk formation. TSH — thyroid hormone secretion. ACTH — glucocorticoids from the adrenal cortex. LH and FSH (gonadotrophins) — in males, LH stimulates androgens from testis; FSH and androgens regulate spermatogenesis. In females, LH induces ovulation of mature Graafian follicles and maintains the corpus luteum; FSH stimulates growth of ovarian follicles.
  • Pars intermedia — only MSH (acts on melanocytes, regulates skin pigmentation); in humans it is almost merged with pars distalis.
  • Pars nervosa stores and releases oxytocin and vasopressin, made in the hypothalamus and transported axonally. Oxytocin — contraction of smooth muscles: vigorous uterine contraction at childbirth and milk ejection. Vasopressin / ADH — water and electrolyte resorption by distal tubules, reducing diuresis. Impaired ADH causes diabetes insipidus (water loss, dehydration).
19.2.3 Pineal, 19.2.4 Thyroid, 19.2.5 Parathyroid, 19.2.6 Thymus ▸
  • Pineal — dorsal side of the forebrain; secretes melatonin, which regulates the 24-hour (diurnal) rhythm — sleep-wake cycle, body temperature; also influences metabolism, pigmentation, the menstrual cycle and defence capability.
  • Thyroid — two lobes on either side of the trachea joined by the isthmus; made of follicles and stromal tissue; follicular cells make thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). Iodine is essential.
    • Iodine deficiency → hypothyroidism and goitre (enlarged thyroid). Hypothyroidism during pregnancy → cretinism in the baby (stunted growth, mental retardation, low IQ, abnormal skin, deaf-mutism). In adult women it may make the menstrual cycle irregular.
    • Hyperthyroidism — from thyroid cancer or nodules. Exophthalmic goitre (Graves' disease) — enlarged thyroid, protruding eyeballs, increased BMR, weight loss.
    • Functions: regulate basal metabolic rate; support RBC formation; control carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism; influence water and electrolyte balance. Thyrocalcitonin (TCT), a protein hormone, regulates (lowers) blood calcium.
  • Parathyroid — four glands on the back of the thyroid; secrete peptide PTH, regulated by circulating Ca2+. PTH increases blood Ca2+: stimulates bone resorption (demineralisation), Ca2+ reabsorption by renal tubules and Ca2+ absorption from food — a hypercalcemic hormone. With TCT, maintains calcium balance.
  • Thymus — lobular, between the lungs behind the sternum, ventral to the aorta; secretes thymosins — differentiation of T-lymphocytes (cell-mediated immunity) and antibody production (humoral immunity). It degenerates in old age, weakening immune responses.
19.2.7 Adrenal & 19.2.8 Pancreas ▸
  • Adrenal glands — one above each kidney; central medulla, outer cortex.
  • Adrenal medulla — adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine) = catecholamines; secreted rapidly in stress and emergencies — emergency / fight-or-flight hormones. Increase alertness, pupillary dilation, piloerection, sweating, heart beat, strength of heart contraction and rate of respiration; stimulate glycogen breakdown (raising blood glucose) and breakdown of lipids and proteins.
  • Adrenal cortex — three layers: zona reticularis (inner), zona fasciculata (middle), zona glomerulosa (outer); secretes corticoids.
    • Glucocorticoids (main: cortisol) — stimulate gluconeogenesis, lipolysis, proteolysis; inhibit cellular uptake of amino acids; maintain cardiovascular system and kidney function; anti-inflammatory, suppress immune response; stimulate RBC production.
    • Mineralocorticoids (main: aldosterone) — act on renal tubules: reabsorb Na+ and water, excrete K+ and phosphate; maintain electrolytes, fluid volume, osmotic pressure and blood pressure.
    • Small amounts of androgenic steroids — axial, pubic and facial hair at puberty.
    • Underproduction of cortical hormones → Addison's disease (altered carbohydrate metabolism, acute weakness, fatigue).
  • Pancreas — composite gland (exocrine + endocrine). Endocrine part: Islets of Langerhans, 1–2 million, only 1–2% of pancreatic tissue. α-cells → glucagon; β-cells → insulin (both peptide hormones).
  • Glucagon — acts on liver cells (hepatocytes): glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, reduces cellular glucose uptake → hyperglycemic hormone.
  • Insulin — acts on hepatocytes and adipocytes: increases cellular glucose uptake and utilisation, and glycogenesis → lowers blood glucose (hypoglycemic). Prolonged hyperglycemia → diabetes mellitus (glucose in urine, harmful ketone bodies); treated with insulin therapy.
19.2.9 Testis, 19.2.10 Ovary & 19.3 Other Hormones ▸
  • Testis — in the scrotal sac; primary sex organ and endocrine gland; seminiferous tubules plus interstitial tissue. Leydig (interstitial) cells secrete androgens, mainly testosterone: development and function of male accessory sex organs (epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate, urethra); muscular growth, facial and axillary hair, aggressiveness, low-pitched voice; stimulate spermatogenesis; influence male sexual behaviour (libido); anabolic effects on protein and carbohydrate metabolism.
  • Ovary — produces one ovum per menstrual cycle and two groups of steroid hormones. Estrogen (mainly from growing follicles): growth of female secondary sex organs, follicle development, female secondary sex characters (e.g. high-pitched voice), mammary gland development, female sexual behaviour. After ovulation the ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, secreting mainly progesterone: supports pregnancy, forms mammary alveoli and milk secretion.
  • Heart — atrial wall secretes ANF (atrial natriuretic factor): when blood pressure rises, it dilates blood vessels and lowers BP.
  • Kidney — juxtaglomerular cells secrete erythropoietin, stimulating RBC formation (erythropoiesis).
  • GI tract — gastrin (gastric glands: HCl and pepsinogen); secretin (exocrine pancreas: water and bicarbonate); CCK, cholecystokinin (pancreatic enzymes and bile); GIP, gastric inhibitory peptide (inhibits gastric secretion and motility). Non-endocrine tissues also secrete growth factors for normal growth and repair.
19.4 Mechanism of Hormone Action ▸
  • Hormones act by binding specific hormone receptors, present only in target tissues: membrane-bound receptors on the cell membrane, or intracellular receptors (mostly nuclear). Binding forms a specific hormone-receptor complex, causing biochemical changes in the target tissue.
  • Chemical groups: (i) peptide, polypeptide, protein hormones (insulin, glucagon, pituitary and hypothalamic hormones); (ii) steroids (cortisol, testosterone, estradiol, progesterone); (iii) iodothyronines (thyroid hormones); (iv) amino-acid derivatives (epinephrine).
  • Hormones acting on membrane-bound receptors usually do not enter the cell; they generate second messengers (cyclic AMP, IP3, Ca++) that regulate cellular metabolism — e.g. protein hormones.
  • Hormones acting on intracellular receptors (steroid hormones, iodothyronines) mostly regulate gene expression or chromosome function through the hormone-receptor complex interacting with the genome.
