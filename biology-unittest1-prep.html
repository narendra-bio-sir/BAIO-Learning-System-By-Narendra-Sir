Chapter 1 · The Living WorldNo chapter page yet — full notes below
1.1 What Is Living? ▸

No single feature defines life on its own — each commonly cited property has an exception, except one.

  • Growth — increase in cell number and mass. In plants it is indeterminate (continues throughout life via meristems); in most animals it stops at maturity. In unicellular organisms, growth and reproduction are the same event — one cell dividing into two. Non-living crystals also “grow” by deposition, so growth alone cannot define life.
  • Reproduction — production of progeny. Not universal either: mules, sterile worker bees, and infertile human couples are alive but do not reproduce.
  • Metabolism — the sum of all chemical reactions in the body. Present in every living organism without exception, which is why many call it the defining feature. But isolated metabolic reactions can be demonstrated outside the body too, in a cell-free system — those reactions are not “alive”, so metabolism alone is necessary but not sufficient.
  • Consciousness — the ability to sense the environment (physical, chemical or biological stimuli) and respond — through behavioural, physiological or other responses. Seen in every living organism without a single exception, from one bacterium to a human. This is the property the chapter ultimately settles on as the most reliable indicator of life, taken together with the others.
1.2 Diversity in the Living World ▸

About 1.7–1.8 million species have been described so far. To study and compare them, every organism needs one scientific name, agreed upon everywhere.

  • Binomial nomenclature, given by Carolus Linnaeus — each organism gets two names: Genus (capitalised) + species (lowercase), both Latinised and italicised (or underlined when handwritten). E.g. Mangifera indica (mango), Homo sapiens (man).
  • Governed by international codes — ICBN for plants, ICZN for animals — fixed rules so the same name means the same organism anywhere in the world.
  • Taxonomy — the process of classification, identification and naming, based on characteristics. Systematics (Latin systema = systematic arrangement) is the wider study that also brings in diversity and evolutionary relationships between organisms.
1.3 Taxonomic Categories ▸

Classification is hierarchical — each rank is called a taxon. From lowest to highest:

  • Species — the most fundamental unit; a group of individuals with basic similarities that can interbreed. E.g. Mangifera indica.
  • Genus — related species sharing more characters with each other than with species of other genera. E.g. Panthera leo (lion) and Panthera tigris (tiger) share genus Panthera.
  • Family — related genera with still fewer shared characters. E.g. Solanaceae (potato, brinjal, tomato); Felidae (lion, tiger, leopard).
  • Order — families with a few characters in common, e.g. based on floral features.
  • Class — orders with common characteristics grouped together, e.g. Primata and Carnivora under class Mammalia.
  • Phylum (animals) / Division (plants) — classes with common characters, e.g. Mammalia, Aves, Reptilia under phylum Chordata.
  • Kingdom — the highest and broadest category, e.g. Kingdom Animalia, Kingdom Plantae.
1.4 Taxonomical Aids ▸
  • Herbarium — dried, pressed plant specimens mounted on sheets, labelled with name, family, date, place and collector — a quick reference store for taxonomic study.
  • Botanical gardens — places that grow living plants for reference and identification, each labelled with its scientific name and family. Famous examples: the Indian Botanical Garden, Howrah, and the National Botanical Research Institute, Lucknow.
  • Museums — preserve plant and animal specimens for study, in preservative solutions or dried; insects are pinned, larger animals are stuffed (taxidermy); skeletons are also kept.
  • Zoological parks — zoos where wild animals are kept in a protected, semi-natural setting so their food habits and behaviour can be studied.
  • Taxonomic key — used to identify specimens using contrasting character pairs (a “lead”); accepting one option and rejecting the other repeatedly narrows down to the correct taxon. A separate key is prepared for each taxonomic category. Related aids: Flora, Manuals, Monographs and Catalogues.

2.1 Five Kingdom Classification & Kingdom Monera ▸

Proposed by R.H. Whittaker (1969), based on cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction and phylogenetic relationships: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia. Viruses, viroids and lichens are left out of all five.

  • Monera — prokaryotic, unicellular. Archaebacteria survive in extreme habitats (methanogens in marshy areas and ruminant guts; halophiles in extreme salt; thermoacidophiles in hot acidic springs) due to a different cell wall structure. Eubacteria (true bacteria) are grouped by nutrition — photosynthetic autotrophs (cyanobacteria / BGA, e.g. Nostoc, Anabaena, some fix atmospheric N2), chemosynthetic autotrophs (oxidise inorganic substances), and heterotrophs (most bacteria — decomposers, a few pathogenic, e.g. citrus canker). Mycoplasma lack a cell wall entirely — the smallest living cells known, many pathogenic.
2.2 Kingdom Protista ▸

Single-celled eukaryotes, mostly aquatic, with a defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

  • Chrysophytes — diatoms and golden algae; photosynthetic; cell walls form indestructible pits (diatomaceous earth).
  • Dinoflagellates — mostly marine, photosynthetic, two flagella (one longitudinal, one transverse), stiff cellulose cell wall plates. The red species Gonyaulax multiplies rapidly to cause red tides, releasing toxins that can kill marine animals.
  • Euglenoids — freshwater, no cell wall (a flexible protein-rich pellicle instead), two flagella. Photosynthetic in sunlight but behave as heterotrophs (predators) when sunlight is unavailable. E.g. Euglena.
  • Slime moulds — saprophytic; the body (plasmodium) creeps over decaying matter engulfing organic material; under adverse conditions forms fruiting bodies with resistant spores dispersed by air.
  • Protozoans — heterotrophic predators or parasites: Amoeboid (Amoeba), Flagellated (Trypanosoma — sleeping sickness), Ciliated (Paramecium), Sporozoans (Plasmodium — malaria).
2.3 Kingdom Fungi ▸

Heterotrophic (saprophytic or parasitic), chitin cell wall, mostly multicellular filaments (hypha, network = mycelium) except unicellular yeast.

  • Phycomycetes — aseptate, coenocytic mycelium; asexual spores in a sporangium; sexual fusion forms a thick-walled zygospore. E.g. Mucor, Rhizopus (bread mould).
  • Ascomycetes — septate mycelium; asexual conidia; sexual ascospores inside sac-like asci. Includes Saccharomyces (yeast) and Penicillium (antibiotics).
  • Basidiomycetes — mushrooms, rusts, smuts; sex organs absent, but fusion of two somatic cells gives a dikaryon, which forms basidia bearing 4 basidiospores each. E.g. Agaricus, Puccinia.
  • Deuteromycetes — “imperfect fungi”; only asexual conidia known; decomposers important in mineral cycling. E.g. Alternaria, Trichoderma.
  • Lichens — a symbiosis of algae (food, via photosynthesis) and fungi (shelter, water/mineral absorption); sensitive indicators of SO2 pollution.
2.4 Viruses & Viroids ▸
  • Viruses — sub-microscopic, non-cellular; genetic material (usually RNA) wrapped in a protein coat (capsid, made of capsomeres); obligate parasites, not alive outside a host cell. E.g. TMV (tobacco mosaic virus).
  • Viroids — discovered by T.O. Diener (1971); smaller than viruses; only free low-molecular-weight RNA, no protein coat. Caused potato spindle tuber disease.

3.1 Algae ▸

Chlorophyll-bearing, simple, thalloid, autotrophic, mostly aquatic — unicellular (Chlamydomonas) to colonial (Volvox) to filamentous (Spirogyra). Three classes, by pigment:

  • Chlorophyceae (green) — grass-green (Chl a & b), fresh water, cellulose wall, starch stored. E.g. Chlamydomonas, Ulothrix, Chara.
  • Phaeophyceae (brown) — marine, fucoxanthin pigment, mannitol/laminarin stored, cellulose+algin wall, gas-filled floats (pneumatocysts). E.g. Fucus, Laminaria, Sargassum.
  • Rhodophyceae (red) — r-phycoerythrin pigment, mostly marine (some at greater depths), floridean starch stored, some encrusted with calcium carbonate (reef builders). E.g. Polysiphonia, Gelidium.
3.2 Bryophytes ▸

“Amphibians of the plant kingdom” — live on land but need water for sexual reproduction. No true roots, stem or leaves; attached by rhizoids.

  • The dominant plant body is the haploid gametophyte; multicellular sex organs — male antheridium (biflagellate antherozoids), female archegonium (one egg).
  • Fertilisation gives a zygote that grows into a dependent sporophyte (not free-living), which produces spores by meiosis; spores germinate into a new gametophyte — this cycling between phases is alternation of generations.
  • Liverworts (e.g. Marchantia) — thalloid body; asexual reproduction by gemmae in gemma cups.
  • Mosses (e.g. Funaria, Sphagnum) — spore germinates into a protonema first, which develops the upright leafy gametophore; Sphagnum (peat moss) is used as fuel, packing material and a soil conditioner.
3.3 Pteridophytes ▸

The first terrestrial plants with true vascular tissue and a differentiated root-stem-leaf body; the sporophyte is dominant (unlike bryophytes).

  • Spore-bearing leaves are sporophylls. Homosporous types (e.g. Equisetum) produce one kind of spore; heterosporous types (e.g. Selaginella, Salvinia) produce microspores and megaspores — retaining the female gametophyte on the parent sporophyte here is the evolutionary lead-in to the seed habit seen in gymnosperms and angiosperms.
  • The spore grows into a small, free-living gametophyte called the prothallus, which still needs water for fertilisation.
3.4 Gymnosperms & Angiosperms ▸
  • Gymnosperms — “naked seed” plants; ovules are not enclosed by an ovary. Heterosporous; microsporophylls and megasporophylls are compacted into cones/strobili. Monoecious (both cones on one plant, e.g. Pinus) or dioecious (Cycas). Roots may show mycorrhiza (Pinus) or N2-fixing coralloid roots (Cycas).
  • Angiosperms — flowering plants; seeds enclosed within a fruit. Stamen produces pollen (male gametophyte); pistil's ovary encloses ovules (female gametophyte/embryo sac). Double fertilisation is unique to this group — one male gamete fertilises the egg (zygote), the other fuses with the secondary nucleus to form the triploid endosperm. Classified by cotyledon number into Dicotyledonae (2) and Monocotyledonae (1).
