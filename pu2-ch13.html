Board Important – Karnataka 2nd PUC Question Bank

Biodiversity and Conservation combines conceptual theory with named examples and numbers – hotspot counts, IUCN categories and conservation strategies are asked almost every year.
~8–10 marksConservation-focused chapter, strong on named examples and numerical facts
3 × 5 MCauses of biodiversity loss, in-situ conservation strategies, ex-situ conservation methods
4 × 3 MSpecies-area relationship, patterns of biodiversity (latitudinal gradient), importance of biodiversity, IUCN Red List categories
4 × 2 MBiodiversity hotspots, endemism, Sacred Groves, Convention on Biological Diversity/Earth Summit

⭐⭐⭐ Top priority – almost always asked (5 marks)

  • Causes of biodiversity loss – the "Evil Quartet" (5 M) – habitat loss & fragmentation (e.g. Amazon rainforest clearing for soybean cultivation/cattle ranches), over-exploitation (e.g. Steller's sea cow, passenger pigeon driven to extinction), alien/invasive species introduction (e.g. Nile perch in Lake Victoria wiped out cichlid fish species; water hyacinth choking water bodies), and co-extinction (when a species' extinction causes extinction of an obligate-associated species, e.g. host fish & unique parasites).
  • In-situ conservation strategies (5 M) – conserving species in their natural habitat: National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries, and Biosphere Reserves (with a core zone of legally protected area and a buffer zone allowing controlled human activity); also includes Sacred Groves (forest patches protected by tribal/local communities on religious grounds, e.g. Karnataka's Kans, Meghalaya, Rajasthan).
  • Ex-situ conservation methods (5 M) – conserving species away from their natural habitat: zoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks (breeding in captivity and re-introduction into the wild, e.g. Gir lion); cryopreservation of gametes of endangered species in gene/seed banks; in vitro fertilisation, tissue culture propagation, and seed banks for long-term storage of seeds of different genetic strains/wild relatives of crops.

⭐⭐ High priority – frequently asked (3 marks)

  • Species-area relationship (3 M) – within a region, species richness (S) and area (A) show a rectangular hyperbola, expressed as log S = log C + Z log A, where Z is the slope (regression coefficient), roughly 0.1–0.2 for smaller areas/continents but steeper, 0.3–0.35, for larger areas or when analysed among very large islands/biogeographic provinces.
  • Latitudinal gradient in biodiversity (3 M) – species diversity generally increases from the poles to the tropics, so tropical regions (e.g. Amazon rainforest, Western Ghats) have far more species than temperate/polar regions, due to more solar energy, less seasonal variation, and more evolutionary time for speciation in the tropics.
  • Importance/ecosystem services of biodiversity (3 M) – provides ecosystem services like oxygen renewal, pollination, prevention of soil erosion, climate regulation; narrow utilitarian value (direct economic benefits – drugs, food, fibre), broad utilitarian value (e.g. Amazon rainforest producing 20% of Earth's oxygen), and ethical/existence value (moral duty to conserve all species).
  • IUCN Red List categories (3 M) – Extinct, Extinct in the Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern; use these categories to classify a species' risk of extinction based on population trends and habitat status.
  • Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)/Earth Summit (3 M) – the 1992 Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit called for measures to conserve biodiversity and sustainable utilisation of its benefits; the 2002 World Summit on Sustainable Development (Johannesburg) reaffirmed commitment to the CBD, aiming to achieve a significant reduction in the current rate of biodiversity loss.

⭐ Two-mark favourites – learn as clean pairs

  • Biodiversity hotspots – regions with very high species richness and endemism, facing serious threat of habitat loss (India has the Western Ghats-Sri Lanka, Himalaya, Indo-Burma, and Sundaland hotspots).
  • Endemic species (found only in a particular geographic region, nowhere else) vs cosmopolitan/widely-distributed species.
  • Alpha diversity (within a habitat) vs beta diversity (between habitats/communities) – both contribute to total (gamma) diversity of a region.
  • National Park (in-situ, high protection, no human activity permitted) vs Biosphere Reserve (in-situ, has a buffer zone allowing regulated human activity).
  • Sacred Groves – forest patches protected by local communities/tribal groups for religious/cultural reasons, an important in-situ conservation practice in India.
Answer-writing tip: for the "Evil Quartet" causes-of-loss question, always name all four causes AND give one real named example for each (e.g. Nile perch for invasive species) – examiners award marks separately for the cause and for the correct example.
Never lose these marks: always remember India's four biodiversity hotspots by name (Western Ghats-Sri Lanka, Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Sundaland) and don't confuse in-situ (National Park/Sanctuary/Biosphere Reserve, in natural habitat) with ex-situ (zoo/botanical garden/seed bank, away from natural habitat).
