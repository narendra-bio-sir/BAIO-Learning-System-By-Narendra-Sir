Extraction & Chromatography of Leaf Pigments

A green leaf is never just "green" — it hides three or four different pigments behind the dominant colour of chlorophyll. Press play to grind a leaf into a pigment extract, spot it onto chromatography paper, and watch the rising solvent pull each pigment apart into its own coloured band.

Aim

To extract the pigments present in a green leaf and to separate them by paper chromatography.

Principle

Leaves contain more than one photosynthetic pigment — chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotene and xanthophyll — all held inside the thylakoid membranes. Because all four are lipid-soluble, grinding the leaf with an organic solvent such as acetone extracts them together into one dark green solution.

Paper chromatography separates this mixture because each pigment has a different solubility in the moving solvent (mobile phase) versus adsorption onto the paper (stationary phase). The less polar pigments travel fastest and farthest with the solvent front, while the more polar ones lag behind. The ratio of the distance a pigment travels to the distance the solvent front travels is its Rf value — a constant, identifying property for a given solvent system.

Method

Requirements

You need: fresh green leaves · mortar and pestle · acetone · chromatography paper (Whatman No. 1) strip · fine capillary tube or dropper · chromatography jar with lid · solvent mixture (petroleum ether : acetone, about 9:1) · pencil & scale

Procedure

  1. Cut a few fresh, healthy green leaves into small pieces and grind them with a little acetone using a mortar and pestle until a dark green extract is obtained.
  2. Filter the extract through a fine cloth into a small, stoppered vial. Keep it away from bright light and heat, as pigments degrade quickly.
  3. Take a strip of chromatography paper and draw a faint pencil line ("origin") about 2 cm from one end.
  4. Using a fine capillary tube, apply a small, concentrated spot of the extract exactly on this line. Let it dry and repeat 8–10 times, keeping the spot as small as possible.
  5. Pour the solvent mixture into the chromatography jar to a shallow depth, and suspend the paper so that the origin stays clearly above the solvent surface — the spot must never touch the solvent directly.
  6. Cover the jar with its lid to keep the atmosphere saturated with solvent vapour, and allow the solvent to rise undisturbed.
  7. When the solvent front is about 2–3 cm from the top, remove the paper and immediately mark the solvent front with a pencil before it evaporates. Allow it to air-dry.

Inference
  • A "green" leaf actually contains at least four distinct pigments — two chlorophylls and two carotenoids — the chlorophylls simply dominate and mask the others until they are separated.
  • The order of separation (carotene > xanthophyll > chlorophyll a > chlorophyll b, top to bottom) reflects decreasing Rf, i.e. decreasing solubility in the non-polar solvent and increasing polarity.
  • Because Rf = (distance travelled by the pigment) ÷ (distance travelled by the solvent front), each pigment has a characteristic, reproducible Rf value for a given solvent system — useful for identifying an unknown pigment.
Precautions
  • Keep the extract and the developing chromatogram away from direct sunlight and heat — chlorophylls degrade (bleach) readily.
  • The pigment spot must be small, concentrated and completely dry, and must never dip directly into the solvent — only the paper below the spot should touch it.
  • Always keep the jar covered so the atmosphere stays saturated with solvent vapour; this keeps the solvent front even and prevents streaking.
  • Mark the solvent front the instant the paper is removed — it evaporates and becomes invisible within minutes.
  • Work in a well-ventilated area, away from any flame — acetone and petroleum ether are volatile and highly flammable.
